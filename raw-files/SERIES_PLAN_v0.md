## EPISODE 1 — R.N. Kao

*(Already scripted in full per your document — included here for series continuity only.)*

**Who:** Rameshwar Nath Kao (10 May 1918 – 20 January 2002), born Varanasi, Kashmiri Pandit family. Joined the Imperial Police, then Intelligence Bureau. Personal security for Nehru, investigated the Kashmir Princess bombing, spent a year helping Ghana build its own intelligence service at Nehru's request. Built the Aviation Research Centre after the 1962 war. Founding Secretary (Research) of RAW, 1968–1977.

**Contribution:** Built RAW from ~250 officers with no doctrine and no public mandate; ran the 1971 intelligence effort supporting Bangladesh's liberation (Mukti Bahini training, coverage of both wings of Pakistan); the "Kaoboys" culture of discretion and professionalism.

**Sources:** Nitin Gokhale, *R.N. Kao: Gentleman Spymaster* (2019, foreword by Ajit Doval); B. Raman, *The Kaoboys of R&AW*; Wikipedia.

## EPISODE 2 — K. Sankaran Nair

**Who:** Kunhiraman Sankaran Nair, known within the service as "Colonel Menon" (born 20 December 1919 or 1920, Ottapalam, Kerala — sources differ by a year; died 17 November 2015, aged 94–96). Joined the Imperial Police in 1943, later the Intelligence Bureau. In 1959 he succeeded Kao as the officer sent to organize Ghana's intelligence service at Kwame Nkrumah's request. Became Kao's deputy when RAW was formed in 1968.

**The specific contribution:** Working under cover as "Colonel Menon," Nair built and ran the network of East Pakistani contacts that fed into the 1971 operation, and is credited with training the Mukti Bahini guerrillas — RAW's covert-war effort, codenamed Operation Jackpot, which trained roughly 83,000 freedom fighters by the war's end. He is also reported to have obtained advance intelligence of Pakistan's planned pre-emptive air strikes in early December 1971, allowing the Indian Air Force to be on alert when the strikes came on December 3 — a genuinely dramatic, verifiable beat for your cold open.

**The organizational story:** After Kao's 1977 retirement, Nair became RAW's second chief — but lasted only three months, resigning when Prime Minister Morarji Desai moved to downgrade and trim the agency (this is the "resignation of principle" from your outline). Indira Gandhi later asked him to help restructure RAW after her 1980 return to power.

**Why he's unknown:** Operated under a cover identity for years even domestically; his own memoir title captures the theme directly.

**Sources:** K. Sankaran Nair, *Inside IB and RAW: The Rolling Stone That Gathered Moss* (his own memoir); Wikipedia; IndiaTVNews profile; M.R. Narayan Swamy's writing on the 1971 war; maddy06 blog's detailed profile (secondary but well-cited).

## EPISODE 3 — Ravindra Kaushik ("Black Tiger")

**Who:** Born 11 April 1952 in Sri Ganganagar, Rajasthan — a border town where residents grew up absorbing Punjabi rhythms from both sides. Spotted by RAW scouts after a college mono-act performance portraying a soldier defying enemy torture. Recruited into RAW in 1973 at age 21.

**The specific contribution:** After roughly two years of training, Kaushik crossed into Pakistan in 1975 under the identity **Nabi Ahmed Shakir**. He enrolled at Karachi University, earned an LLB, and — going well beyond his original brief — joined the **Pakistan Army**, eventually rising to the rank of **Major** in the Military Accounts Department. Between 1979 and 1983 he fed a steady stream of high-level intelligence back to India, reportedly including details on Pakistan's nuclear facility at Kahuta. Indira Gandhi is said to have personally given him the title "Black Tiger" in recognition of his work. He also married a Pakistani woman, Amanat, and had a son, deepening his cover to a degree few deep-cover operatives in any country's history have matched.

**The turn:** In 1983, a RAW operative named Inayat Masih, sent to make contact with Kaushik, was captured and, under interrogation, revealed his identity. Kaushik was arrested, tried by a closed military court, and in 1985 sentenced to death — later commuted to life imprisonment. He spent roughly 16–18 years in Pakistani prisons (Sialkot, Kot Lakhpat, and finally Mianwali), reportedly never confirming his identity despite the pressure. He died on **21 November 2001** in Mianwali Central Jail from pulmonary tuberculosis complicated by heart disease, worsened by nearly two decades of neglect.

**Why he's unknown / the "disowned" angle:** Several accounts (notably a widely-shared essay titled "The Forgotten Hero") argue India never formally acknowledged or fought for his release, and his death passed with almost no public notice at the time. This is your emotional gut-punch episode — treat the "abandonment" framing carefully, since it's a strong claim made mostly by op-eds and secondary retellings rather than an official Indian government admission; present it as the widely reported narrative rather than an established fact.

**Recent pop-culture tie-in:** Kaushik's story is frequently cited as the real-life basis claimed for elements of Bollywood's "Tiger" franchise, and was referenced again around the 2025–26 film *Dhurandhar* — useful for a hook if you want to open with "you may have seen a fictionalized version of this man already."

**Sources:** Wikipedia; ThePrint, "The cost of being an Indian spy"; Economic Times slideshow; multiple 2025–26 retrospective pieces (OpIndia, NewsX, Zee News, Deccan Chronicle) published around *Dhurandhar*'s release — cross-check dates/details across these since several are clearly derivative of each other rather than independent reporting.

## EPISODE 4 — B. Raman

**Who:** Bahukutumbi Raman (1936 – 16 June 2013). Joined the Indian Police Service in 1961 (Madhya Pradesh cadre), moved into the Intelligence Bureau's External Intelligence Division in 1967, and was among the first officers Kao recruited when RAW was formed in 1968 — a genuine founding-generation figure. Never married.

**The specific contribution:** Headed RAW's **Counter-Terrorism Division from 1988 to 1994**, overseeing intelligence on Pakistan-backed jihadist networks and Sikh militancy during one of the most violent periods of the Punjab insurgency. His work also touched Operation Meghdoot (the 1984 Siachen Glacier operation) and RAW's involvement during the Sri Lankan civil war. Retired as Additional Secretary, Cabinet Secretariat, on 31 August 1994.

**The chronicler role — the real hook for this episode:** After retirement, Raman became India's most prolific public voice on intelligence and terrorism — testifying before the US House Armed Services Committee (2002) and a House International Relations sub-committee (2003), writing for the South Asia Analysis Group, and authoring four books, most importantly ***The Kaoboys of R&AW: Down Memory Lane*** (2007) — the single most-reviewed book that year on Indian intelligence, and one of only two full books ever written about Kao-era RAW (the other being Gokhale's Kao biography). He deployed to Mumbai immediately after the 1993 blasts for post-incident assessment work.

**Why this episode matters structurally:** Raman is the bridge between your terror-network series and this new RAW series — he was the man professionally responsible for understanding exactly the kind of ISI-linked networks your first four episodes cover, from the other side of the desk.

**Sources:** Wikipedia; Goodreads (Kaoboys of R&AW reception); Grokipedia profile (detailed but verify against primary sources given the platform); Garuda Books author page; his own published works.

## EPISODE 5 — N.F. Suntook

**Who:** N.F. Suntook, from the Indian Frontier Administrative Service (not IPS) — one of only two non-police-officer chiefs RAW ever had (the other being Vikram Sood, Episode 8). Served in the Navy, then police service, then the Frontier Administrative Service before intelligence work. Worked in India's Northeast and helped train intelligence agencies in Africa.

**The specific contribution:** Founded the **Radio Research Centre (RRC)** and the **Electronics and Technical Services (ETS)** — RAW's signals-intelligence and electronic-intelligence backbone, still structurally part of the agency today. Became RAW's third chief in 1977, right after Kao and Nair's departures — appointed, per multiple accounts, at a deliberately *lower rank* than Kao had held, reflecting the political downgrading of the agency under Morarji Desai. He is credited with essentially **saving RAW from being dismantled** by a government hostile to it in the immediate post-Emergency period, before Indira Gandhi's 1980 return restored the agency's mandate. In 1983, Indira Gandhi asked him to undertake one final sensitive mission to Mauritius during a political crisis there, just as he was set to retire.

**The distinctive angle:** Where Kao is remembered as the visionary founder, Suntook's story is about institutional survival during an agency's most vulnerable political moment — a genuinely different kind of contribution (defensive rather than expansive), and one that pairs well thematically with Nair's principled resignation in Episode 2.

**Sources:** Wikipedia (RAW organizational table); DailyO retrospective on "21 spymasters"; Yatish Yadav, *RAW: A History of India's Covert Operations*; Shashank Mattoo's researched X/Twitter thread on the Mauritius mission (secondary, but well-sourced — verify key claims against Yadav's book before scripting).

## EPISODE 6 — G.B.S. Sidhu & the Sikkim Team

**Who:** G.B.S. Sidhu, head of the three-man RAW team stationed in Gangtok, Sikkim, in the early-to-mid 1970s. Published his own account in 2018: ***Sikkim: Dawn of Democracy — The Truth Behind the Merger With India***.

**The specific contribution — and the framing challenge:** Sidhu's team was tasked with building a united political opposition inside Sikkim to pressure the ruling Chogyal (monarch) toward union with India. Per Sidhu's own book and independent historical accounts (Sunanda Datta-Ray's *Smash and Grab: The Annexation of Sikkim*; B.S. Das's *The Sikkim Saga*), RAW's local team instigated and guided the anti-Chogyal agitation, organized public meetings and processions in Gangtok, and helped plan the April 1975 disarming of the Sikkim Guards — the monarch's loyal soldiers — ahead of the merger vote. Sikkim became India's 22nd state in May 1975.

**Handle this one carefully in the script:** Indian government framing has historically called this a peaceful, popular "merger." Independent historians (Datta-Ray in particular) and Sikkimese accounts describe it as an engineered "annexation." A 2024–25 book, Jackie Hiltz's *A Kingdom Remembered*, explicitly notes there is still no widely accepted, even-handed account of how it happened — this is a genuinely contested history, not a settled one, and the script should present it that way rather than adopting either side's framing as fact.

**The "three women" narrative device:** Multiple sources (Das's own book) frame the story around three women — Indira Gandhi, Hope Cooke (the Chogyal's American wife), and "Kazini" Elisa Maria (British wife of the pro-merger politician Kazi Lhendup Dorji) — a genuinely useful narrative structure for the episode if you want a hook beyond "here's what RAW did."

**Sources:** G.B.S. Sidhu, *Sikkim: Dawn of Democracy* (Penguin, 2018); Sunanda Datta-Ray, *Smash and Grab: Annexation of Sikkim*; B.S. Das, *The Sikkim Saga* (1983); Jackie Hiltz, *A Kingdom Remembered* (via The Wire's 2025 review); Eurasia Review/Islamicity op-eds summarizing Sidhu's book (secondary — use for narrative color, verify factual claims against Sidhu's book directly where possible).

## EPISODE 7 — A.K. Verma

**Who:** A.K. Verma, an ex-Intelligence Bureau officer who became RAW's sixth chief, serving 1987–1990.

**The specific contribution:** Two major operations under his tenure:
- **Operation Cactus (1988)** — India's rapid military/intelligence response to a coup attempt against the government of the Maldives, generally regarded as one of independent India's cleanest successful external interventions.
- The **Indian Peace Keeping Force (IPKF)** deployment to Sri Lanka — a much more complicated legacy, since the IPKF's mission against the LTTE became bogged down and controversial, eventually leading to a costly and criticized Indian military commitment.
- Continued RAW's counter-ISI covert operations in Punjab; B. Raman later wrote that these operations were "responsible for ending the Khalistani insurgency" but were discontinued (and never restarted) during a later government's tenure — a claim from a former insider that should be flagged as an insider's assessment, not independently verified fact.

**The narrative shape:** This episode pairs a clean success (Cactus) against a much messier, contested legacy (IPKF) under the same chief — good material for a more nuanced "success and failure both flow from good intelligence work" episode, different in tone from the more unambiguously heroic earlier episodes.

**Sources:** Wikipedia RAW organizational history; Yatish Yadav, *RAW: A History of India's Covert Operations* (direct quotes on Bangladesh coup analysis and Afghan mujahideen assessments during his tenure); B. Raman's own writings on Punjab covert operations (South Asia Analysis Group archives).

## EPISODE 8 — Vikram Sood

**Who:** Vikram Sood, from the Indian Postal Service (not police) — the second of only two non-IPS chiefs RAW has had. Headed RAW from December 2000 to March 2003, succeeding A.S. Dulat.

**The specific contribution:** Founded the **National Technical Facilities Organisation** (a precursor/component of what later fed into India's broader technical-intelligence architecture). His tenure covered the immediate post-9/11 period and the 2001–02 India-Pakistan military standoff following the Parliament attack — meaning he was RAW's chief during one of the most dangerous stretches of the modern India-Pakistan relationship.

**The "second act" that makes him distinctive:** Unlike most former chiefs who largely retreated from public life, Sood became one of India's most prolific and widely-read intelligence commentators — authoring *The Unending Game* (2018), *The Ultimate Goal* (2020), and *Great Power Games* (2025), consistently arguing publicly for RAW's urgent modernization, especially in cyber and digital-era espionage. This gives you a natural "still relevant today" closing beat other episodes don't have.

**Sources:** Wikipedia; The Quint's review of *The Unending Game*; Observer Research Foundation (his current institutional home) author page; Amazon/publisher listings for his three books.

## EPISODE 9 — A.S. Dulat

**Who:** Amarjit Singh Dulat, RAW chief from 1999 to his retirement in December 2000.

**The specific contribution — the direct tie-in to your terror series:** Dulat was RAW's chief during the **IC-814 hijacking crisis** (December 1999) — the exact event your series flagged earlier as JeM founder Masood Azhar's origin story, since Azhar's release was the price paid to end the siege. Dulat has spoken candidly and publicly (including a notable September 2024 interview) about the indecision and "goof-ups" in the government's handling of the crisis — a rare instance of a former chief openly criticizing the response on the record rather than defending it.

**The second act:** After RAW, Dulat became a Kashmir interlocutor and wrote *Kashmir: The Vajpayee Years* — widely read, and notably co-authored a later book with a former ISI chief (A.S. Dulat and Asad Durrani's *The Spy Chronicles*), an unusual and controversial format: two rival intelligence chiefs writing candidly together.

**Why this episode is valuable for your series specifically:** It closes the loop on IC-814/Masood Azhar from the Indian intelligence side, giving your audience the "what was actually happening in the room" counterpart to the hijacking story, without you needing a full separate IC-814 episode (which you've noted is oversaturated content elsewhere).

**Sources:** Wikipedia; India Today, September 2024 interview on IC-814 "indecisions and goof-ups"; DailyO piece on "what Dulat left out of his book"; Deccan Herald interview on Kashmir; his own books.

## EPISODE 10 — Rabinder Singh (the cautionary-tale finale)

**Who:** Rabinder Singh, a RAW officer who defected to the United States in 2004, reportedly having worked for years as a CIA source inside the agency before his exfiltration.

**Why this closes the series well:** Every episode so far is a story of contribution, sacrifice, or institutional resilience. A finale built around an actual espionage failure — a mole inside RAW itself, successfully exfiltrated by a foreign intelligence service — gives the series a note of honesty and consequence it would otherwise lack, and raises exactly the "why does the public barely know this name" question your format asks, but for the opposite reason: this one, the agency has strong incentive to keep quiet.

**Handle with care:** Details of this case are far thinner in open-source reporting than any other episode on this list — most available material is fragmentary Indian press coverage from 2004 with limited follow-up, and some claims (the mechanics of his exfiltration specifically) verge into territory your project should avoid per your own "no operational tradecraft" rule. **This episode needs dedicated research before scripting** — flag it to me separately when you're ready and I'll do a focused search pass rather than relying on the fragments above.

**Alternative if research comes up too thin:** Consider swapping in a better-documented modern figure — Rajinder Khanna (founder of RAW's counter-terrorism unit, later Additional NSA) is a well-documented, currently-serving-adjacent figure who'd give you a "the work continues today" closing note instead of a scandal note, if you'd rather end the series on continuity than caution.
