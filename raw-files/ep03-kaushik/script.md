# THE BLACK TIGER: Ravindra Kaushik
### RAW Files · Episode 3 — long-form documentary script

**Runtime target:** 10–12 minutes
**Tone:** Investigative, restrained, human. Pro-India point of view; Indian sources first (The Telegraph's 2002 report, Hindustan Times, The Pioneer, Times of India, ThePrint). Facts from `research.md`; what rests on the family alone is said to rest on the family. No tradecraft. Through-line: the spy who saw everything in Pakistan — and asked India for one line of recognition.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:30)

**[VISUAL: A folded letter in Hindi on a dark table; a Pakistani jail wall; an Indian family home in Rajasthan.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
A prisoner in a Pakistani jail is dying of tuberculosis. Three days before the end, he writes one last letter home. [PAUSE] According to the Hindustan Times, one line reads: "Had I been an American, I would have been out of this jail in three days."

He is an Indian. He has spent more than sixteen years in Pakistani custody. He was arrested as a spy, sentenced to death, and, by his family's account, never gave up who he really was.

His name was Ravindra Kaushik. The Indian government has never officially said he worked for it. [PAUSE] So who was this man, and what did he give India that was worth a life?

Everything here comes from Indian newspapers, and from the family that fought to be heard. The Telegraph reported his story in 2002. ThePrint revisited it in 2023. [PAUSE] It begins on a college stage in Rajasthan.

**[TITLE CARD: THE BLACK TIGER]**

## SEGMENT 1 — THE MIMIC OF SRI GANGANAGAR (1:30–3:00)

**[VISUAL: Dossier portrait — Ravindra Kaushik (silhouette; the only photograph is non-free). Sri Ganganagar station; a college stage.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Ravindra Kaushik was born on April 11, 1952, in Sri Ganganagar, a border town in Rajasthan. His father had served in the Indian Air Force. He studied commerce at a local college, and became known for two things: mimicry, and mono-acting.

He grew up between the wars of 1965 and 1971, in a town close to the border. His brother, R.N. Kaushik, later told Hindustan Times what he thought caught the eye of India's intelligence men. It was, he said, "probably his mono-act in college, in which he played an Indian army officer who refused to divulge information to China." [PAUSE]

He was playing a man who would not talk under interrogation. [PAUSE] Years later, he would have to be that man.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Ravindra Kaushik · born Sri Ganganagar, Rajasthan, 11 April 1952"]**

## SEGMENT 2 — PROJECT X (3:00–4:45)

**[VISUAL: Checklist — "Project X · 1970s" — the purpose, the office, the output, the shutdown.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In the mid-1970s, India was nervous. General Zia-ul-Haq had taken power in Pakistan in 1977, and India feared he might go to war. According to ThePrint's Praveen Swami, citing R&AW officers, the agency ran a secret effort called Project X. Its aim was to place long-term agents at the heart of Pakistan's establishment, to give India early warning of any military mobilisation.

Kaushik was recruited in the early 1970s, and spent about two years in training in Delhi. He went across in 1975, under a new name: Nabi Ahmed Shakir. [PAUSE] His own family says he even studied law at Karachi University.

From there, the accounts split. His family says he joined the Pakistan Army as a commissioned officer, and rose to the rank of Major. R&AW's own officers, speaking to ThePrint, say he took a clerical job in the Army's military accounts service. [PAUSE] Either way, he sat in the right office.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — ThePrint, "The cost of being an Indian spy"; The Pioneer; The Telegraph]**

## SEGMENT 3 — WHAT HE SAW (4:45–6:15)

**[VISUAL: A quiet office ledger; then a montage of Pakistani landmarks from the 1970s; a Pakistan Army crest placeholder card.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
What did a clerk in that office see? Movements of military units. Postings of key officers. Even the movement of trains carrying war material. [PAUSE] ThePrint quotes R&AW officers calling it "a goldmine for the agency."

Some retellings go much further. They say he gave India details of Pakistan's nuclear programme, or its war plans in Punjab. No primary source supports any of it. R&AW's file on him, ThePrint notes, has never been declassified. [PAUSE] India's own officers will not say which battle he changed. They say only that the material was gold.

The family knew nothing of the detail. But they knew he was alive. According to his nephew, he came home three or four times. And in Pakistan, by the family's account, he married and had a child. [PAUSE] He was living a second life so deep that it had a second family.

His mother, Amladevi, later wrote to Prime Minister Vajpayee. A spy sitting in the right place, she said, could save the lives of at least twenty thousand soldiers of his nation.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Project X — early warning of military mobilisation · Kaushik's cover name: Nabi Ahmed Shakir"]**

## SEGMENT 4 — 1983 (6:15–7:45)

**[VISUAL: A park bench; an arrest; Sialkot; a sealed courtroom.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In 1983, it ended. R&AW sent another man to make contact with Kaushik. His name was Inayat Masih. He was caught. According to The Telegraph, in the interrogation that followed, he gave Kaushik away. [PAUSE] Kaushik was twenty-nine.

ThePrint notes that R&AW's own files do not conclusively explain how Pakistan's counter-intelligence found him. Some officers suspect it was not a single mistake. [PAUSE] Whatever the cause, the man who was never supposed to be seen had been seen.

He was held at an interrogation centre in Sialkot. The Telegraph reports he was tortured for two years. In 1985, a closed military court sentenced him to death. The sentence was later reduced to life.

After that, the prisons. Sialkot. Kot Lakhpat. Mianwali. Different reports name different jails. All agree on the length: about sixteen years. [PAUSE] And through all of it, his family says, he never gave up his real name.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — The Telegraph, December 2002; Hindustan Times, December 2009]**

## SEGMENT 5 — THE LETTERS (7:45–9:15)

**[VISUAL: Quote card — "Kya Bharat jaise bade desh ke liye kurbani dene waalon ko yahi milta hai?" Then a jail-visit mailbag; the family home in Jaipur.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In prison, he wrote home. His letters, smuggled out, reached his family in Rajasthan. In one, quoted by The Telegraph, he asked a question in Hindi. "Kya Bharat jaise bade desh ke liye kurbani dene waalon ko yahi milta hai?" [PAUSE] "Is this the reward for those who sacrifice their lives for a country as big as India?"

His family wrote again and again to the government. From 1987, The Telegraph says, they received the same reply: the case had been taken up with Pakistan. [PAUSE] Nothing more.

In November 2001, according to The Telegraph, Ravindra Kaushik died in Mianwali jail. The cause was tuberculosis and heart disease. Other reports give a different jail, and a different year. His family remembers the pain, not the date. [PAUSE] His father did not long outlive him.

For years afterwards, the government sent his parents a monthly allowance: five hundred rupees, later two thousand. His brother Rajeshwarnath told Hindustan Times: "He will always remain important for me. But for the country, he was just another agent."

**[LOWER THIRD: "Died in a Pakistani jail, November 2001 (The Telegraph) · aged 49"]**

---

## SEGMENT 6 — THE NAME (9:15–10:45)

**[VISUAL: Karan Johar's poster is not shown; instead, a newspaper headline card — "India's forgotten spy" — and a Bollywood film title card.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The name "Black Tiger" has stuck to him ever since. His family says it was given by Indira Gandhi herself. [PAUSE] No document supports that. It is a family claim, first heard in 2012.

That year, his nephew took the makers of the film Ek Tha Tiger to court. He believed the story was his uncle's. The director, Kabir Khan, said he had only read about Kaushik in the papers. It was "an interesting story," he said, "but it's definitely not the story of my film."

Kaushik was not the only one. ThePrint lists other Indian agents who came home after years in Pakistani jails, and found no recognition. One of them, Kishori Lal Sharma, said: "Even if you escape death, you die a slow death as nobody is there to own you."

His brother's demand was small. [PAUSE] "We don't want money," he told The Telegraph. "What we want from the government is recognition."

## CLOSING (10:45–11:45)

**[VISUAL: The folded letter from the cold open; the dark photo frame; the silhouette; the Indian tricolour on a blank wall.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
A student pretended to be an army officer who would not talk. Years later he became a man who could not tell anyone who he was. [PAUSE] He never did.

There is no memorial to the agents who gave their lives. There is no official photograph. There is a letter, from a Pakistani jail, asking why.

Ravindra Kaushik gave India its early warning, and asked for one line in return. [PAUSE] India still owes it to him.
