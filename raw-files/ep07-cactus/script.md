# SIXTEEN HOURS: A.K. Verma and Operation Cactus
### RAW Files · Episode 7 — long-form documentary script

**Runtime target:** 10–12 minutes
**Tone:** Investigative, proud, honest. Pro-India point of view: the rescue at an elected president's request, and peacekeeping at Colombo's request, are India's account and lead the script. Indian sources first (Indian Express, India Today, Scroll's excerpt from Sushant Singh's "Mission Overseas", DailyO, Rediff, Bharat Rakshak, Brig. Dhillon's recollections). Facts from `research.md`. No operational methods; the political and strategic story only.

## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:30)

**[VISUAL: A telephone receiver off its cradle; the PMO at 7 Race Course Road; South Block.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
November 3, 1988. Morning, in Delhi. A telephone in the Prime Minister's Office is ringing. On the line is the Foreign Secretary of the Maldives, calling from a house in Malé that looks straight at the exchange the rebels are trying to seize. [PAUSE] An Indian official tells him: don't hang up.

For the next eighteen hours, according to Sushant Singh's account, that line stays open. Two receivers, one in Malé and one in Rajiv Gandhi's office, lie off their cradles. It is India's best source of information about the coup.

Meanwhile, in South Block, R&AW reports to a room full of generals that the airport in Malé has fallen. It has not. [PAUSE] That, too, is in Sushant Singh's account.

This is the story of Operation Cactus. It is one of the cleanest interventions in India's history. It is also a story about what intelligence could not do. [PAUSE] The chief of R&AW at the time was A.K. Verma. Everything here is from Indian sources, in India's words. It starts with a president who was supposed to visit Delhi.

## SEGMENT 1 — THE PRESIDENT WHO STAYED HOME (1:30–3:00)

**[VISUAL: Region map, Malé and the Maldives; Maumoon Abdul Gayoom; a presidential motorcade card.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Maumoon Abdul Gayoom had been President of the Maldives since 1978. In September 1988 he was elected to a third term. He was due to be sworn in on November 11.

On November 3, he was supposed to be in India. The visit was cancelled while his aircraft was still in the air, because Rajiv Gandhi had to leave Delhi to campaign. According to Lt Gen Vinod Bhatia, the plotters had known the schedule. "They staged the coup when the President of Maldives was to come to India." [PAUSE] He was at home instead.

At about four in the morning, gunfire broke out at the headquarters of the Maldivian National Security Service. A force of Sri Lankan Tamil mercenaries, from the group PLOTE, had come ashore by speedboat from a hijacked freighter. In most Indian accounts, there were about eighty.

Corporal Hussain Adam held the main gate of the barracks until his ammunition ran out. He was killed there. [PAUSE] He is still remembered in the Maldives as a hero.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — The Indian Express, "Operation Cactus"; WION, Lt Gen Vinod Bhatia]**

## SEGMENT 2 — THE CALL (3:00–4:30)

**[VISUAL: Checklist — the morning of 3 November: the appeals · the answers · the decision.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
By morning, Gayoom had been moved to a safe house. Through one surviving phone line, his Foreign Minister and Foreign Secretary began ringing the world. The United States said its bases were too far away. The United Kingdom could not reach in time. Sri Lanka and Pakistan could not or would not help. [PAUSE] And India said yes.

Ronen Sen, in the Prime Minister's Office, phoned the Army Chief, General V.N. Sharma. Could the Army help? "Of course we can help, Ronen." At around nine, Rajiv Gandhi chaired a meeting in the Army's operations room at South Block. According to Sen, "No one had any clear ideas, and people were passing around tourist picture postcards of the Maldives."

That was the intelligence. A picture postcard.

At the same meeting, R&AW reported that Malé's airport had been taken. According to Sushant Singh, it had not, and the report was shut down. [PAUSE] The country's best picture of Malé was still a diplomat's telephone.

## SEGMENT 3 — GENTLEMEN, WE WILL LAND (4:30–6:15)

**[VISUAL: Agra airfield; Brigadier F.F.C. Bulsara; an IL-76 taking off; the runway at Hulhule, unlit.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The job fell to the 50th Independent Parachute Brigade at Agra. Its commander was Brigadier F.F.C. Bulsara.

At the briefing, someone proposed dropping into a football stadium. A young officer, Major Dhillon, objected. According to Dhillon's later account, Bulsara said, "Malik, sit down, I will take it from here." His plan was simple: land the first company, and jump if the airport was hostile. [PAUSE] On the speaker, the Army's Vice-Chief answered. "OK. It's your funeral!"

Bulsara put down the phone. "Gentlemen, we will land!"

Two Ilyushin IL-76 transports took off from Agra in the late afternoon. They flew about 2,600 kilometres over the Indian Ocean, more than four hours, in the dark, to a foreign airport that no one could see. [PAUSE] At 9:48 in the evening, the first aircraft touched down on an unlit runway, guided by a code word, "Hudiya."

The runway lights came on for a few seconds. That was enough.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Agra to Hulhule · about 2,600 km · first landing 9:48 p.m., 3 November 1988"]**

## SEGMENT 4 — MALÉ (6:15–7:45)

**[VISUAL: Hulhule airport; speedboats crossing the harbour; the NSS headquarters; a photograph of Gayoom with the paras.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Within minutes of landing, the paratroopers took the airport. They crossed the water to Malé by speedboat. By the early hours of November 4, Colonel Subhash Joshi and Major Dhillon had found President Gayoom, safe, in a servants' quarters, and brought him to the security headquarters.

At about four in the morning, Gayoom spoke to Rajiv Gandhi. Dhillon later recalled a patch through the Indian attaché in Colombo. A voice said, "This is Rajiv." Dhillon, believing it was a duty officer, said the President of the Maldives wanted to speak to the Prime Minister. [PAUSE] "Major, I am the PM!"

In Parliament, Rajiv Gandhi reported that the Maldives had "appealed to us in desperation in its grave hour of need." He said India's troops had carried out their task "in an exemplary fashion, in the highest traditions of the Indian Armed Forces."

From the first alert in Delhi to Gayoom being secured, Indian sources put the time at under sixteen hours. [PAUSE] There was one Indian casualty: a soldier who, reportedly, shot himself in the foot.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — The Indian Express, November 2021; India Sentinels, Brig. R.J.S. Dhillon; Bharat Rakshak]**

## SEGMENT 5 — THE CHASE (7:45–9:00)

**[VISUAL: The freighter MV Progress Light; INS Godavari and INS Betwa; the Indian Navy's maritime patrol aircraft.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The mercenaries fled. They took a freighter, MV Progress Light, with hostages aboard, among them a Maldivian minister. The Indian Navy gave chase.

INS Betwa left Cochin. INS Godavari, returning from a long voyage to Australia, was turned around. Maritime aircraft kept the ship in sight. [PAUSE] It was about six knots, and it had nowhere to go.

On November 6, India Today reported, the Navy fired warning shots, and the mercenaries surrendered. Some hostages had already been killed. By the time the ship was taken, the coup was over.

Gayoom had his country back. A part of the Indian force, 6 Para, stayed on for a year, at his request. [PAUSE] The rest went home within two weeks.

## SEGMENT 6 — THE MAN IN THE ROOM (9:00–10:30)

**[VISUAL: Dossier portrait — A.K. Verma (silhouette; no free photograph). R&AW emblem; Rajiv Gandhi; a China card.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
And Verma? Anand Kumar Verma was a Madhya Pradesh policeman, from the 1953 batch. He took over R&AW in June 1987. No Indian source credits him with a specific role in Cactus.

What he is credited with is different. According to DailyO, citing B. Raman, Verma gave R&AW back its teeth. It had been cut back after 1977. Now it was allowed to answer Pakistan's sponsorship of terror in Punjab. Raman would later write that the agency's ability to raise the cost of that interference was "little known and understood."

Verma also opened quiet channels. In 1988 he met Pakistan's intelligence chief, Hamid Gul, at a meeting in Jordan. And, before Rajiv Gandhi's December visit to China, he opened a line to the Chinese leadership, when India's foreign ministry had advised against the trip. His deputy, Vappala Balachandran, called it "perhaps the highest point in our organisation's record."

In February 1989, Rajiv Gandhi sent him to Afghanistan's capital. Western governments were telling India to pull its diplomats out. Verma reported that Najibullah's government could hold on. It lasted three more years. [PAUSE] He was right.

---

## SEGMENT 7 — THESE ARE OUR BOYS (10:30–11:45)

**[VISUAL: Sri Lanka; the IPKF; Jaffna University; a quote card — "These are our boys."]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The same period had a darker side. In July 1987, the Indo-Sri Lanka Accord brought Indian peacekeepers to Sri Lanka, at President Jayewardene's request. Within months, they were fighting the Tigers, the LTTE, whom Delhi had once sheltered.

In October 1987, a heliborne assault on a university in the Tamil north went wrong. A hundred and three commandos and thirty infantrymen were flown in. Six commandos did not return. None of the infantry did. [PAUSE] A further thirty-five died in the rescue.

India's High Commissioner in Colombo, J.N. Dixit, later told Rediff that a senior intelligence voice had assured the government: "These are our boys. Once they have agreed, they will not betray." Dixit added: "Intelligence agencies did not analyse it from that point of view at all." [PAUSE] He did not name anyone. "Why blame one or the other?" he said. "All of us who were involved are to be blamed."

It is the shadow behind Cactus. In Malé, once again, the picture was wrong. [PAUSE] This time, the Army made up for it.

## CLOSING (11:45–12:45)

**[VISUAL: The paratroopers on the Hulhule runway; the open telephone; the silhouette.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
India's first great act of force across the Indian Ocean was won by a prime minister who decided in hours, a brigadier who overruled his headquarters, and pilots who landed on a runway they could not see. [PAUSE] It was not won by a spy.

The spy chief's work happened elsewhere. In Punjab, and in the corridors of foreign capitals. He was the man in the room when India learned what it could do. [PAUSE] And he gave the intelligence service back what it had lost.

Sixteen hours from a phone call to a president saved. It began with one line, left open. [PAUSE] No one had hung up.
