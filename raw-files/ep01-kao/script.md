# THE SPYMASTER: R.N. Kao and the Birth of RAW
### RAW Files · Episode 1 — long-form documentary script

**Runtime target:** 14–16 minutes
**Tone:** Investigative, admiring, precise. Pro-India point of view; Indian sources first (Nitin Gokhale's biography, B. Raman, ThePrint, The Tribune, Swarajya). Facts from `research.md`; single-source claims marked "reportedly". Through-line: one quiet officer, and the country he kept safe without ever being seen.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:30)

**[VISUAL: World map. A route from Hong Kong towards Bandung; the line stops over the South China Sea.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
April 11, 1955. An Air India airliner, the Kashmir Princess, leaves Hong Kong carrying Chinese delegates to a summit in Indonesia. Somewhere over the South China Sea, a bomb in the wing goes off. [PAUSE] Sixteen people die. The man the bomb was meant for, China's Premier Zhou Enlai, is not on board.

Prime Minister Nehru needs to know who did it. He does not send a diplomat. He sends one young police officer from the Intelligence Bureau. Six months later, that officer is sitting across from Zhou Enlai in Beijing, with the answer.

His name was Rameshwar Nath Kao. He would go on to build India's foreign intelligence service from nothing, help a new nation be born, and see India gain its twenty-second state. [PAUSE] And almost no photograph of him exists.

So how does a man shape the map of South Asia and stay almost invisible doing it? This is the story of the Research and Analysis Wing's founder — told, as far as possible, by the people who worked beside him. [PAUSE] It starts with that plane.

**[TITLE CARD: THE SPYMASTER]**

## SEGMENT 1 — THE PLANE MEANT FOR ZHOU (1:30–3:00)

**[VISUAL: The Air India Constellation; Zhou Enlai at Bandung; Kai Tak airport, 1950s.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The Kashmir Princess was a Lockheed Constellation, chartered to fly the Chinese delegation to the Bandung Conference. Only three of its crew survived. [PAUSE] Zhou Enlai had changed his plans at the last minute.

Kao's inquiry took him across Asia — Hong Kong, Singapore, Indonesia. According to The Tribune's profile of him, he traced the bomb to a ground worker at Hong Kong's Kai Tak airport, recruited by the Kuomintang — the Chinese Nationalists on Taiwan. The worker had reportedly been promised six hundred thousand Hong Kong dollars.

Zhou Enlai was impressed enough to send Kao a letter of appreciation. [PAUSE] A thirty-seven-year-old Indian police officer had solved an assassination plot against the Premier of China.

Nehru noticed. From then on, when India needed something done quietly, the name that came up was Kao.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — The Tribune, 2014 profile; Wikipedia, "Kashmir Princess"]**

## SEGMENT 2 — THE POLICEMAN (3:00–4:30)

**[VISUAL: Dossier portrait — R.N. Kao (silhouette; no free photograph exists). Varanasi, 1920s; Nehru, 1957.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
He was born in Benares, now Varanasi, on May 10, 1918, into a Kashmiri Pandit family. He studied English literature at Allahabad University. Nothing about him suggested a spy. In 1940 he joined the Indian Police. [PAUSE] Seven years later, weeks before independence, he moved to the Intelligence Bureau.

There he became a protector of leaders. He ran security for Prime Minister Nehru, and for visiting heads of state — among them, in 1961, Queen Elizabeth II. [PAUSE] Nobody noticed him. That was the job.

By 1958 India trusted him with something unusual. Ghana had just won its independence from Britain, and its founding president, Kwame Nkrumah, asked India for help. The Intelligence Bureau's chief, B.N. Mullik, picked Kao. He went to Accra and helped build Ghana's own foreign intelligence service from scratch. [PAUSE] Ten years before India had one of its own.

He was, by every account, courteous and soft-spoken. Former National Security Adviser M.K. Narayanan later told ThePrint that Kao "pulsated warmth and was essentially a father figure." [PAUSE] He was also, colleagues say, very hard to fool.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Rameshwar Nath Kao · born Varanasi, 10 May 1918 · Indian Police 1940 · Intelligence Bureau 1947"]**

## SEGMENT 3 — FROM NOTHING (4:30–6:15)

**[VISUAL: Checklist — 1962 war · Aviation Research Centre · 1965 war · 21 Sept 1968 · R&AW founded.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Then came 1962. China's army crossed into India, and India was caught unprepared. The war exposed how little the country knew about what lay beyond its borders.

Kao was given the job of seeing further. He became the first head of the Aviation Research Centre at Charbatia in Odisha, India's new arm for aerial and technical intelligence. [PAUSE] It was not yet an agency. But it was the first building block.

After the 1965 war with Pakistan, Prime Minister Indira Gandhi decided India needed a separate service for foreign intelligence, answering directly to her. The Research and Analysis Wing was born on September 21, 1968. It sat inside the Cabinet Secretariat. Kao was its first chief.

It began with roughly 250 people and a budget of about two crore rupees. Within a few years, both had grown many times over. Kao hand-picked his officers, and demanded two things above all — discretion and professionalism. [PAUSE] Inside the service, they came to be known as the "Kaoboys." According to B. Raman, one of the first of them, several would go on to lead Indian intelligence themselves.

**[LOWER THIRD: "21 September 1968 — R&AW founded · first chief: R.N. Kao"]**

## SEGMENT 4 — THE KAOBOYS (6:15–7:30)

**[VISUAL: A strip of name cards — K. Sankaran Nair · P.N. Banerjee · M.B.K. Nair · B. Raman — and the R&AW emblem.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Kao's real talent was people. He recruited beyond the usual police channels, and built what India had never had. There were desks watching each neighbour. There was a technical wing for aerial and signals intelligence, grown out of Charbatia. Later came a small Policy and Research Staff, which his biographer Nitin Gokhale calls a forerunner of today's National Security Council Secretariat.

Inside the service, discretion was everything. [PAUSE] Few of its officers were ever named in public.

Foreign services noticed. Former Joint Intelligence Committee chairman K.N. Daruwala said Kao's contacts across Asia "were something else." Within three years of its founding, the new agency would face a test that most intelligence services never get. [PAUSE] A war that would redraw the map.

## SEGMENT 5 — THE NOTE (7:30–9:45)

**[VISUAL: A document card — the 14 January 1971 note. Then Indira Gandhi with the service chiefs; the Instrument of Surrender.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In January 1971, Kao sent a twenty-five-page secret note to the Cabinet Secretary. Its warning, published in Nitin Gokhale's biography, was blunt. There would be "a temptation for Yahya Khan to consider the prospects of embarking on a military venture against India." [PAUSE] Pakistan's dictator, Kao was saying, might start a war.

Weeks later, an Indian Airlines plane was hijacked from Srinagar to Lahore. India answered by banning Pakistani flights over its territory. At a stroke, the air link between West and East Pakistan was cut — a response Indian accounts credit to Kao's planning. [PAUSE] Not a shot had been fired.

On the night of March 25, Pakistan's army launched its crackdown in East Pakistan. Sheikh Mujibur Rahman was arrested. Millions of refugees poured into India. [PAUSE] Three weeks later, a Bangladesh government-in-exile was formed, with Calcutta as its base.

R&AW's teams there were led by P.N. Banerjee, with Kao's deputy K. Sankaran Nair running the Pakistan desk. They backed the Bengali freedom fighters of the Mukti Bahini — with training, supplies and intelligence. Indian accounts put the number of fighters trained at up to a hundred thousand. [PAUSE] By winter, Pakistan's army in the east was fighting a war it could not see.

On December 3, Pakistan's air force struck Indian airfields. [PAUSE] India struck back. Thirteen days later, in Dhaka, Pakistan's eastern command surrendered. [PAUSE] A new country, Bangladesh, was born.

Years afterwards, according to ThePrint, Bangladesh's future president Ziaur Rahman told Indira Gandhi that Kao "knows more about my country than me."

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Swarajya, Kao's January 1971 note; DailyO; ThePrint, "RAW at 50"]**

## SEGMENT 6 — TEN DAYS (9:45–11:00)

**[VISUAL: The Chogyal of Sikkim; Gangtok's Tsuklakhang; region map with a pin on Gangtok.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In late 1972, Indira Gandhi showed Kao a letter from the Chogyal, Sikkim's ruler. Sikkim sat on a narrow strip between Nepal, Tibet and Bhutan, above the corridor that links India to its north-east. According to R&AW officer G.B.S. Sidhu, writing in The Tribune, she asked Kao one question. [PAUSE] "Can you do something about this?"

Kao asked for two weeks. He came back in ten days, with a yes.

What followed was politics, not war. Sikkim's people had long been demanding democracy. In 1974 they voted overwhelmingly for pro-India parties. In April 1975, a referendum returned about ninety-seven percent in favour of joining India. In May, Sikkim became India's twenty-second state. [PAUSE] Critics called it an annexation. Sikkim's voters had called it a choice.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Sikkim — referendum 14 April 1975 · India's 22nd state, May 1975"]**

## SEGMENT 7 — THE COST OF TRUST (11:00–12:45)

**[VISUAL: Sheikh Mujibur Rahman; Morarji Desai; a falling budget bar; Rajiv Gandhi.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Intelligence only works if leaders listen. In 1975, according to ThePrint, Kao personally warned Bangladesh's founding leader, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, that a coup was being planned against him. [PAUSE] Mujib did not act on it. On August 15, 1975, he and most of his family were killed.

Two years later, it was Kao's own service that paid for a leader's distrust. In 1977, Morarji Desai became Prime Minister. Journalist Asoka Raina wrote that Desai "was contemptuous of RAW and he believed that it was just another police force." Budgets and operations were cut. Kao stepped down.

An inquiry later cleared him of wrongdoing, according to ThePrint. And the exile did not last. When Indira Gandhi returned in 1980, she brought Kao back as her senior security adviser. After her assassination in 1984, he advised Rajiv Gandhi, and his groundwork is credited in the creation of the National Security Guard. That same year, according to M.K. Narayanan, Kao quietly visited China, preparing the ground for Rajiv Gandhi's landmark trip to Beijing in 1988. [PAUSE] Twenty-nine years after the Kashmir Princess, he was back where the story began.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — ThePrint, "RAW at 50"; Indian Defence Review, "How We Killed the Kaoboys"]**

## SEGMENT 8 — THE MAN NOBODY PHOTOGRAPHED (12:45–14:00)

**[VISUAL: An empty photo frame. Then the R.N. Kao Memorial Lecture, 2026.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Kao died in Delhi on January 20, 2002. He was eighty-three. He never wrote a memoir. He almost never gave interviews. He avoided cameras, even at family weddings. [PAUSE] Some of his papers remain restricted, on his own instructions.

Abroad, his peers knew exactly who he was. The head of French intelligence, Alexandre de Marenches, was quoted admiring "a fascinating mix of physical and mental elegance." At home? [PAUSE] Almost nothing. His first full biography, by Nitin Gokhale, appeared only in 2019. That was seventeen years after his death.

Today R&AW holds a memorial lecture in his name every year. [PAUSE] The most famous Indian spy is still a man most Indians would not recognise.

**[LOWER THIRD: "R.N. Kao · 1918–2002 · founder, Research and Analysis Wing"]**

---

## CLOSING (14:00–15:00)

**[VISUAL: The Kashmir Princess route from the cold open; the dossier frame stays empty; the R&AW emblem.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In 1955, one police officer found the men who tried to kill China's Premier. In 1968 he built an agency. In 1971 he helped a nation be born, and by 1975 India had a twenty-second state. [PAUSE] Almost none of it carries his face.

That was the point. A spy who is seen has already failed. R.N. Kao left India a service, a culture, and a generation of Kaoboys who carried it on.

He left almost no picture of himself. [PAUSE] Just the map he helped redraw.
