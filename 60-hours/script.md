# 60 HOURS: The Hour-by-Hour Story of 26/11
### Long-form documentary script — YouTube (explainer/investigative format)

**Runtime target:** 12–14 minutes
**Tone:** Cinematic, forensic, taut. Restructured from the original draft (`script_draft_v0.md`) to `../SCRIPT_RULES.md` — same facts and sources, new architecture.

## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:00)

**[VISUAL: Black screen. A digital clock ticks in: 21:20. Then a map of South Mumbai, five points still dark.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
9:20 PM, November 26, 2008. [PAUSE] Ten men step off inflatable speedboats onto the Mumbai shoreline. Each has a rifle. Each has a phone.

In the next ten minutes, five attacks begin across the city. Over the next sixty hours, this becomes the longest hostage siege India has ever faced. [PAUSE] But here is the part that still doesn't fit. The men pulling the triggers weren't the ones in charge. The orders came down a phone line, from a room six hundred kilometers away — where someone was watching the siege on live television.

So who was really holding Mumbai hostage?

**[TITLE CARD: 60 HOURS]**

## SEGMENT 1 — THE BOAT NO ONE SAW (1:00–2:30)

**[VISUAL: Map. A line traces from Karachi, out into the Arabian Sea, toward Mumbai.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Everything that follows comes from the record — police and NSG timelines, court testimony, and the recordings themselves. And the record says this story doesn't start on November 26th. [PAUSE] It starts five days earlier.

On the night of November 21st, ten men slipped out of Karachi aboard two cargo vessels, the Al Fauz and the Al Hussaini. For roughly thirty-eight hours, the Indian Navy never saw them. Each man carried an AK-47 and eight grenades. And dried fruit, for the journey.

On November 23rd, at around 4:30 PM, they seized an Indian fishing trawler, the MV Kuber. They killed four of its crew. The captain, they kept alive — to sail them to India. [PAUSE] Within sight of Mumbai, they killed him too. No one was left who could warn anyone.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Nov 21–26 — Karachi to Mumbai — 38 hours at sea, hijacked trawler"]**

Except the warnings already existed. Indian intelligence had intercepted a satellite call on November 18th about a sea-borne attack. US intelligence had warned the Taj Hotels chairman, Ratan Tata, months before. [PAUSE] Two warnings. Neither one changed what was waiting on the shore.

## SEGMENT 2 — TEN MINUTES (2:30–4:30)

**[VISUAL: The Mumbai map. Five pins light up in quick succession, each with a timestamp.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
They came ashore between 8:00 and 8:30 PM. [PAUSE] Two landing points on Cuffe Parade: Macchimar Nagar and Badhwar Park. When curious locals asked who they were, they said they were students. [PAUSE] Then they split into pairs.

Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus. One of the busiest railway stations on earth. Ajmal Kasab and his partner, Ismail Khan, walked into the passenger hall and opened fire. They threw grenades into the crowd. Fifty-eight people were killed here. [PAUSE] It was the deadliest single site of the entire attack.

Leopold Café, a few minutes south. Two gunmen sprayed it with bullets and killed ten people. Then they fled on foot. The explosives they'd left in two taxis killed five more.

And at almost the same moment, pairs of gunmen walked into the Taj Mahal Palace and the Oberoi-Trident. Two five-star hotels that would become the main stage for three days. [PAUSE] The fifth target was one most of the world had never heard of: a small Jewish outreach centre called Nariman House.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Wikipedia "Timeline of the 2008 Mumbai attacks" (NDTV/Evening Standard/BBC sourcing); Modern War Institute at West Point case study]**

Five locations. Ten minutes. Ten men. [PAUSE] This is what eighteen months of rehearsal looks like — the curriculum from our last file, finally deployed. But rehearsal alone doesn't explain what happened next.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE VOICE IN KARACHI (4:30–6:00)

**[VISUAL: Split screen motif — a hotel room on one side, a dim room with monitors and a phone on the other. No faces, just silhouettes and static.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The gunmen weren't improvising. [PAUSE] They were being directed.

Intercepted wireless communications show them taking real-time instructions from a control room in Pakistan. That room was watching Indian television news — tracking troop movements and hostage positions, then relaying it back to the men inside the hotels. The siege was being broadcast. And the broadcast was being used as intelligence. When Indian authorities realised it, they cut the television feeds to the hotels.

David Headley later testified that he recognised the voices in that room. His own handler, Sajid Mir. [PAUSE] And two others, known as Abu Khafa and Abu Al Kama. Recordings played in court reportedly captured the control room telling the gunmen that Mumbai's anti-terrorism chief had just been killed. News they heard from Karachi — about something happening a few kilometres away, outside Cama Hospital.

**[LOWER THIRD: "The Karachi control room — identified by Headley as Sajid Mir, Abu Khafa, Abu Al Kama"]**

---

## SEGMENT 4 — THE ONE WHO LIVED (6:00–7:30)

**[VISUAL: Map pin shifts to Cama Hospital.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
From CST, Kasab and Ismail Khan moved on foot to Cama Hospital. It was around 10:30 PM. The staff saw them coming. They locked patients into their rooms. [PAUSE] That decision likely saved lives.

Outside, three of Mumbai's most senior officers tried to stop them from a jeep: Anti-Terrorism Squad chief Hemant Karkare, additional commissioner Ashok Kamte, and senior inspector Vijay Salaskar. All three were killed. The gunmen took the same police jeep and drove off. They didn't get far. Near Girgaum Chowpatty, officers from the Gamdevi police station stopped the jeep. Ismail Khan was killed. [PAUSE] Kasab, wounded, was taken alive.

He was the only one of the ten who would be. And that one survivor is the reason the world would later hear, in his own words, how the operation was built. By 1:00 AM, this thread of the night was over. [PAUSE] The other four were just getting started.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Wikipedia Timeline (NDTV/BBC); Business Standard 15-year retrospective]**

---

## SEGMENT 5 — THE FIRE (7:30–8:45)

**[VISUAL: Map pin on the Taj. Animated flames motif on the dome, subtle, not graphic.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Four gunmen had entered the Taj at around 9:44 PM. They moved through the lobby and up toward the grand staircase. According to a West Point case study of the siege, it was a position that let them watch the main entrance and everything below it. [PAUSE] They had chosen the high ground.

By 11:00 PM, police had surrounded the building. Then, at 1:00 AM, a blast tore through the central dome. Fire spread through the heritage wing. Army units arrived by 2:30 AM, and fire engines by three. Through the dark, firefighters raised ladders and pulled more than two hundred guests to safety while gun battles went on inside.

By dawn, the fire was largely under control. [PAUSE] The siege was not. The gunmen had moved into the newer tower — holding somewhere between one hundred and one hundred and fifty hostages.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Taj Mahal Palace — Night 1 — 200+ evacuated by ladder, gunmen dug into the tower"]**

---

## SEGMENT 6 — THE WAIT (8:45–10:15)

**[VISUAL: Three-way split map — Taj, Oberoi-Trident, Nariman House, all pulsing simultaneously.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
On the morning of the 27th came a decision that has been criticised ever since. Local police were told to stand down and hand full authority to the National Security Guard — India's federal counter-terrorism force. [PAUSE] Its commandos had to fly in from Delhi. [PAUSE] The city waited.

At the Taj, NSG commandos were inside by around 7:20 that evening. Until then, the fighting came in bursts: small evacuations, sudden gunfire, a fire on the fourth floor.

At the Oberoi-Trident, the NSG arrived around 5:30 PM. A naval helicopter surveyed the building from above. Roughly 250 guests would be brought out of this hotel alone, in waves through the night.

At Nariman House, there was nothing to negotiate. Two gunmen held the rabbi, his wife, and several others. The NSG would not move in until the following morning.

By the end of the second day, none of the three sieges was over. [PAUSE] Twenty-four hours, and no end in sight.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Wikipedia Timeline; Modern War Institute at West Point; Business Standard]**

---

## SEGMENT 7 — THE PRICE (10:15–11:45)

**[VISUAL: Map — Oberoi and Nariman House pins turn from red to grey, one after another.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The 28th is when two of the sieges finally end. [PAUSE] Neither ends the way anyone hoped.

At Nariman House, just after 7:30 in the morning, NSG commandos fast-roped from helicopters onto the roof, with snipers covering them from nearby buildings. It took most of the day. By 7:30 in the evening, they had found all six hostages — the rabbi and his wife among them — murdered by their captors. An hour later, both gunmen were dead, and the siege was declared over.

At the Oberoi-Trident, the operation ended around 3:00 PM. Officials counted one hundred and forty-three hostages rescued alive. Twenty-four bodies were recovered. Both terrorists inside were killed.

The Taj, though, was still burning. Six more bodies were recovered that afternoon, followed by ten grenade blasts between 2:53 and 3:59 PM. By evening, officials believed only one gunman was left inside. [PAUSE] They were wrong.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Nov 28 — Nariman House and Oberoi-Trident sieges end — the Taj holds out into Day 3"]**

---

## SEGMENT 8 — THE LAST MAN (11:45–13:00)

**[VISUAL: Map — only the Taj pin remains lit, pulsing faster.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In the early hours of the 29th, the Taj shook again. Five explosions, between 3:40 and 4:10 AM. Ten minutes later, officials declared the hotel under government control. [PAUSE] By 5:05, they had taken it back.

At 7:30 AM, fire was still burning on the first floor. Black smoke rose from the second. Witnesses described a gun battle still under way. Then, sometime between 8:00 and 8:50 AM — accounts differ by minutes — commandos declared the hotel finally, fully secured. The NSG called it Operation Tornado.

**[LOWER THIRD: "8:50 AM, Nov 29 — Taj declared secure — 60 hours after the first shots at CST"]**

From the first shots at CST to the last all-clear at the Taj, the siege had run just under sixty hours.

---

## SEGMENT 9 — THE COST (13:00–14:15)

**[VISUAL: Neutral, respectful graphic — a simple list of numbers on a dark background, no imagery.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
By the official count, 166 people were killed. More than a dozen of them were security personnel, and at least 26 were foreign nationals. More than 300 were injured. [PAUSE] Nine of the ten attackers died. Only Ajmal Kasab survived — tried, convicted, and executed in 2012.

On 12 February 2009, Pakistan's government admitted something for the first time. [PAUSE] The attack had been planned and launched, at least in part, from Pakistani soil. It continued to deny any official state involvement. It denied, specifically, any role for its intelligence services — a role multiple foreign investigators have alleged.

The city didn't wait for answers. CST was running again within hours. Leopold Café was reportedly serving customers within four days. [PAUSE] The Taj reopened to guests within about a month.

---

## CLOSING (14:15–15:00)

**[VISUAL: Slow pull-out from the map, all pins now dark. Fade to black.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
It began with ten men stepping off boats, each with a rifle and a phone. [PAUSE] Nine of them are dead. The tenth was hanged.

But the phone had two ends. On the other one sat the voice in Karachi — the handlers, the trainers, the same structure we followed in our last file, watching the siege on television and steering it in real time. [PAUSE] So after sixty hours, who was really holding Mumbai?
