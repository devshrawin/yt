# 60 HOURS: The Hour-by-Hour Story of 26/11
### Long-form documentary script — YouTube (explainer/investigative format)

**Runtime target:** 20–24 minutes
**Tone:** Measured, chronological, evidence-led. Every timestamp below is sourced to police/NSG timelines, court testimony, and contemporaneous reporting — footnotes at the end.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:00)

**[VISUAL: Black screen. A digital clock ticks in: 21:20. Then a map of South Mumbai, five points still dark.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
9:20 PM, November 26, 2008. Ten men step off three inflatable speedboats onto the Mumbai shoreline. In the next ten minutes, five separate attacks begin at five different locations across the city — and none of the men pulling the triggers are working alone. Every one of them has a phone in his pocket, and on the other end of that phone, in a control room six hundred kilometers away in Karachi, someone is watching television and telling them exactly what to do next.

Over the next sixty hours, this will become the longest hostage siege India has ever faced.

**[TITLE CARD: 60 HOURS]**

---

## SEGMENT 1 — THE 38-HOUR VOYAGE (1:00–3:30)

**[VISUAL: Map. A line traces from Karachi, out into the Arabian Sea, toward Mumbai.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
This story doesn't start on November 26th. It starts five days earlier.

On the night of November 21st, ten men left Karachi aboard two cargo vessels, the *Al Fauz* and the *Al Hussaini*, sailing for roughly thirty-eight hours undetected by the Indian Navy. Each man carried the same kit: six to seven rifle magazines, several hundred loose rounds, eight hand grenades, an AK-47, a pistol, cash, credit cards, and dried fruit for the journey.

On November 23rd, at around 4:30 PM, they hijacked an Indian fishing trawler, the *MV Kuber* — killing four of its crew and forcing the captain to sail them toward the Indian coast. By the evening of the 26th, within sight of Mumbai, they killed the captain too. There was no one left alive who could tell anyone they were coming.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Nov 21–26 — Karachi to Mumbai — 38-hour voyage, hijacked trawler"]**

Indian intelligence, it's worth noting, had actually intercepted a satellite call on November 18th referencing plans for a sea-borne attack — and separately, US intelligence had warned Taj Hotels chairman Ratan Tata months earlier of a potential threat to the hotel. In both cases, the warning didn't translate into the kind of security posture that could stop what came next.

## SEGMENT 2 — 9:20 PM: FIVE ATTACKS, TEN MINUTES (3:30–8:00)

**[VISUAL: The Mumbai map. Five pins light up in quick succession, each with a timestamp.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
At around 8:00 to 8:30 PM, the ten men split into pairs at two landing points along Cuffe Parade — Macchimar Nagar and Badhwar Park — telling curious locals they were students. Within the hour, they'd fanned out across South Mumbai. What happened next unfolded in near-simultaneous fashion, starting at approximately 9:20 PM.

**Chhatrapati Shivaji Terminus.** Ajmal Kasab and his partner, Ismail Khan, walked into the passenger hall of one of the world's busiest railway stations and opened fire with AK-47s, throwing grenades into the crowd. Fifty-eight people were killed and over a hundred wounded in this single location alone — the deadliest single site of the entire attack.

**Leopold Café.** Two gunmen sprayed the popular tourist café with bullets, killing ten, before fleeing on foot. They also left explosives in two taxis nearby, which detonated separately, killing five more.

**The Taj Mahal Palace Hotel, the Oberoi-Trident, and Nariman House.** At almost exactly the same time as CST and Leopold's, pairs of gunmen entered all three of these locations — the two five-star hotels that would become the siege's main stage for the next three days, and a small Jewish outreach center that most of the world hadn't heard of before that night.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Wikipedia "Timeline of the 2008 Mumbai attacks" (NDTV/Evening Standard/BBC sourcing); Modern War Institute at West Point case study]**

Five locations. Ten minutes. Ten men. This is what a coordinated, trained, pre-planned assault looks like when it's executed by people who've rehearsed it — which, per court testimony we covered in our last video, is exactly what these men had spent up to eighteen months doing.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE VOICE IN KARACHI (8:00–10:30)

**[VISUAL: Split screen motif — a hotel room on one side, a dim room with monitors and a phone on the other. No faces, just silhouettes and static.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Here's the detail that turns this from a terrorist attack into something closer to a live-directed military operation.

The gunmen weren't improvising. According to intercepted wireless communications, they were receiving real-time instructions from a control room in Pakistan — a room that was itself watching Indian television news to track troop movements and hostage positions, then relaying that information back to the men inside the hotels. When Indian authorities realized this was happening, they cut television feeds to the hotels.

David Headley later testified in court that he could identify the voices in that control room — naming his own handler, Sajid Mir, along with two others referred to as Abu Khafa and Abu Al Kama, as the men coordinating the ten gunmen from Karachi. Recordings played in court reportedly captured the control room informing the gunmen, in close to real time, that Mumbai's anti-terrorism chief and two senior police officers had just been killed — a reference to what was happening, at that same hour, a few kilometers away outside Cama Hospital.

**[LOWER THIRD: "The Karachi control room — identified by Headley as Sajid Mir, Abu Khafa, Abu Al Kama"]**

---

## SEGMENT 4 — 10:30 PM: CAMA HOSPITAL AND THE DEATH OF HEMANT KARKARE (10:30–13:00)

**[VISUAL: Map pin shifts to Cama Hospital.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
After CST, Kasab and Ismail Khan moved on foot to Cama Hospital, arriving around 10:30 PM. Hospital staff, seeing them approach, locked patients into their rooms — a decision that likely saved lives.

Outside, Mumbai's Anti-Terrorism Squad chief, Hemant Karkare, along with additional commissioner Ashok Kamte and senior inspector Vijay Salaskar, attempted to intercept the gunmen in a jeep. All three were killed in the exchange. The gunmen then hijacked that same police jeep and drove off — only to be intercepted a short distance away near Girgaum Chowpatty by officers from the Gamdevi police station. Ismail Khan was killed in that final exchange. Kasab, wounded, was captured alive — becoming the only one of the ten attackers taken into custody rather than killed.

By roughly 1:00 AM, less than four hours after the attack began, the CST-Cama-Chowpatty thread of the story was essentially over. The other four locations were just getting started.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Wikipedia Timeline (NDTV/BBC); Business Standard 15-year retrospective]**

## SEGMENT 5 — THE TAJ: NIGHT ONE (13:00–17:00)

**[VISUAL: Map pin on the Taj. Animated flames motif on the dome, subtle, not graphic.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Four gunmen entered the Taj Mahal Palace Hotel at around 9:44 PM, moving through the lobby and up toward the hotel's grand staircase — a position that let them control the main entrance and watch movement below, according to a detailed West Point case study of the siege.

By 11:00 PM, Mumbai police had surrounded the building. At 1:00 AM, a massive blast tore through the hotel's central dome, and fire began spreading through the heritage wing. Army units arrived by 2:30 AM, fire engines by 3:00. Through the early hours, firefighters used ladders to pull more than two hundred guests to safety while gun battles continued inside.

By dawn, the picture was this: the fire was largely under control, but the terrorists — now believed to have moved into the hotel's newer tower — were holding somewhere between one hundred and one hundred fifty hostages.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Taj Mahal Palace — Night 1 — 200+ evacuated by ladder, terrorists dug into the tower"]**

---

## SEGMENT 6 — NOVEMBER 27: THE LONGEST DAY (17:00–21:00)

**[VISUAL: Three-way split map — Taj, Oberoi-Trident, Nariman House, all pulsing simultaneously.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The morning of the 27th brought the government's official response into full gear — and, simultaneously, a decision that's been criticized ever since: local police were told to stand down and hand full authority to the National Security Guard, India's federal counter-terrorism force, whose commandos had to fly in from Delhi.

**At the Taj**, National Security Guard commandos entered the building by around 7:20 PM that evening. Fighting continued in bursts through the day — small evacuations, sudden gunfire, a fire set on the fourth floor.

**At the Oberoi-Trident**, NSG commandos arrived around 5:30 PM. Roughly 250 guests would eventually be evacuated from this hotel alone, in waves through the night. A naval helicopter conducted an aerial survey of the building.

**At Nariman House**, the small Jewish outreach center was, by some accounts, less a hostage negotiation than a fixed siege from the outset — the two gunmen inside had taken the rabbi, his wife, and several others hostage, and NSG commandos didn't move in until the following morning.

By the end of November 27th, none of the three sieges had been resolved. The city had now gone more than twenty-four hours without an end in sight.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Wikipedia Timeline; Modern War Institute at West Point; Business Standard]**

---

## SEGMENT 7 — NOVEMBER 28: TWO SIEGES END (21:00–24:30)

**[VISUAL: Map — Oberoi and Nariman House pins turn from red to grey, one after another.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The 28th is when the story starts to resolve — for two of the three remaining sites, at least.

**Nariman House**, just after 7:30 AM, NSG commandos fast-roped from helicopters directly onto the building's roof — one of the operation's most visually dramatic moments, with snipers positioned in nearby buildings to cover the insertion. It took most of the day. By 7:30 PM, commandos found all six hostages inside — including the rabbi and his wife — had been murdered by their captors. An hour later, both gunmen were killed in a final exchange, and the siege was declared over.

**The Oberoi-Trident**, commando operations concluded around 3:00 PM. Twenty-four bodies were recovered; 143 hostages had been rescued alive over the course of the siege; both terrorists inside were killed.

**The Taj**, though, was still going. Six more bodies were recovered that afternoon, followed by a cluster of ten grenade explosions between roughly 2:53 and 3:59 PM. By evening, officials believed only one gunman remained inside — but that estimate would prove premature.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Nov 28 — Nariman House and Oberoi-Trident sieges end — Taj siege continues into Day 3"]**

---

## SEGMENT 8 — NOVEMBER 29: OPERATION TORNADO (24:30–27:00)

**[VISUAL: Map — only the Taj pin remains lit, pulsing faster.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The final hours. Between roughly 3:40 and 4:10 AM on the 29th, five more explosions rocked the Taj. Ten minutes later, officials briefly declared the hotel under government control — only to revise that estimate back down to one remaining gunman by 5:05 AM.

By 7:30 AM, fire was still burning on the first floor, black smoke rising from the second, and what witnesses described as an active gun battle underway. It wasn't until roughly 8:00 to 8:50 AM — accounts vary by a few minutes — that Indian commandos declared the Taj Mahal Palace Hotel finally, fully secured, the last of the attackers killed in what the NSG called Operation Tornado.

**[LOWER THIRD: "8:50 AM, Nov 29 — Taj declared secure — 60 hours after the first shots at CST"]**

From the first shots at CST at 9:20 PM on the 26th, to the final all-clear at the Taj on the morning of the 29th, the siege had run just under sixty hours.

---

## SEGMENT 9 — THE COST, AND WHAT CAME AFTER (27:00–29:30)

**[VISUAL: Neutral, respectful graphic — a simple list of numbers on a dark background, no imagery.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
By the time it was over, 166 people were dead, including more than a dozen security personnel and at least 26 foreign nationals, with more than 300 injured. Nine of the ten attackers were killed. Only Ajmal Kasab survived — tried, convicted, and executed in 2012.

Among the dead were three of Mumbai's most senior police officers, killed in the opening hours near Cama Hospital. On February 12, 2009, Pakistan's government publicly acknowledged for the first time that the attack had been planned and launched, at least in part, from Pakistani soil — while continuing to deny any official state involvement, and specifically denying involvement by its intelligence services, which multiple foreign investigators have alleged played some supporting role.

The Taj reopened to guests within about a month. The train station at CST was, remarkably, functioning again within hours. Leopold Café was reportedly serving customers again within four days.

## CLOSING (29:30–31:00)

**[VISUAL: Slow pull-out from the map, all pins now dark. Fade to black.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
What makes 26/11 different from most terrorist attacks isn't just its scale — it's that it was, in effect, remote-controlled. Ten men on the ground, and a voice on a phone six hundred kilometers away, watching the same news broadcasts everyone else in the world was watching, and using them to direct a live siege in real time.

The men who built that system — the trainers, the handlers, the voice in the Karachi control room — are the same organizational structure we walked through in our last video. This is what that structure produces when it's finally deployed.
