# THE FRONT MAN: The Tahawwur Rana Story — original draft (v0, as shared)

Kept for reference. The restructured, lint-passing script is `script.md`.

## COLD OPEN
In 2011, a jury in Chicago looked at the same man and reached two different verdicts. Not guilty of helping plan the Mumbai attacks. Guilty of supporting the terrorist group that carried them out.
That contradiction sat, mostly unresolved, for fourteen years. Then in April 2025, it became the reason India could finally bring him home to stand trial for the very thing an American jury had already cleared him of.
This is the story of the man who gave a terrorist his cover story — and how a legal technicality became, eventually, his undoing.

## SEGMENT 1 — TWO BOYS AT HASAN ABDAL
Every account of this story — court documents, PBS's Frontline investigation, interviews with Rana's own wife — starts in the same place: a military prep school in Punjab called Cadet College Hasan Abdal, sometime in the late 1970s.
Tahawwur Rana was the son of a school principal. The other boy — born Daood Gilani, later known to the world as David Coleman Headley — stood out immediately: an American mother, a Pakistani father, and, according to classmates, two different colored eyes. The two became close. Decades later, Rana's wife Samraz would tell a PBS documentary crew that the family considered Headley one of their own — he called her "sister," and her children called him "special uncle."
That friendship is the entire structure of this story. Everything that follows — the cover business, the Mumbai office, the trip eleven days before the attack — runs through a decades-old bond between two schoolboys who ended up on opposite continents but never stopped trusting each other.

## SEGMENT 2 — FROM ARMY DOCTOR TO IMMIGRATION CONSULTANT
Rana's own path looked nothing like a terrorist's. He went to medical school, then served as a Captain in the Pakistan Army Medical Corps. Sometime in the late 1990s, he deserted — the exact reasons are still murky — and emigrated to Canada with his wife, also a physician, in 1997. Both became naturalized Canadian citizens in 2001.
From Toronto, he moved into business, eventually settling in Chicago. He built a modest but real portfolio: a halal meat business on Devon Avenue, and — the business that matters here — First World Immigration Services, with offices in Chicago, New York, Toronto, and eventually Mumbai.
This is the part worth sitting with: Rana wasn't running a front organization from day one. He was running an actual immigration consultancy that actual clients used. That's precisely what made it useful cover.

## SEGMENT 3 — THE MUMBAI OFFICE
According to Indian investigators, Headley used the Mumbai branch of First World Immigration as his cover story for repeated scouting trips into India — traveling as a legitimate immigration consultant while actually surveilling targets for Lashkar-e-Taiba, on assignment from Hafiz Saeed and Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi.
Rana himself traveled to India roughly eleven days before the attack, staying at the Taj Mahal Palace Hotel — one of the exact buildings that would be under siege days later. NIA records place him on similar consultant-cover trips to Agra, Kochi, and Hapur.
And here's the detail that separates "unwitting friend" from "informed participant": in early 2009, months after the attack, Headley called Rana in Chicago specifically to let him listen to an intercepted recording — senior LeT commanders directing the ten gunmen in real time. One voice in particular impressed Rana. It belonged to Sajid Mir — one of the network's most senior, and to this day still-absconding, alleged masterminds. Maharashtra's Anti-Terrorism Squad says it holds recordings of Rana and Headley afterward congratulating LeT leadership on the operation's "excellent planning and execution."

## SEGMENT 4 — THE OTHER PLOT: DENMARK
Mumbai wasn't the only operation running through this friendship. In parallel, Headley and Rana were pulled into a plan targeting the Danish newspaper Jyllands-Posten, which had published cartoons of the Prophet Muhammad in 2005. Court documents describe a plan that included beheading newspaper staff.
That plot is, ironically, what caught them. On October 18, 2009 — almost exactly a year after Mumbai — FBI agents arrested both men at O'Hare International Airport in Chicago.

## SEGMENT 5 — THE VERDICT THAT SPLIT THE STORY IN TWO
Headley pleaded guilty in March 2010 and became the prosecution's star witness against his oldest friend. Rana's own trial ran three weeks in Chicago, May into June 2011.
The verdict split cleanly down the middle. The jury acquitted Rana of conspiracy to support the Mumbai attacks themselves — the actual event this entire story is about. But they convicted him on the Denmark plot and on providing material support to LeT more broadly. Fourteen years, plus five years of supervised release. Judge Harry Leinenweber called it, on the record, "a dastardly plot."
Rana's defense throughout was that he'd been manipulated — that he believed Headley's assignments came with the blessing of Pakistan's ISI, not a terrorist network acting on its own. Prosecutors produced emails, wiretaps, and Rana's own post-arrest statements to argue otherwise. The jury, in the end, seems to have split the difference: not convinced enough for Mumbai specifically, convinced enough for everything around it.

## SEGMENT 6 — WHY AN AMERICAN ACQUITTAL DIDN'T END THE STORY
Here's the legal hinge the entire second half of this story turns on. Rana was acquitted of Mumbai conspiracy in the US — but he was never tried in the US on India's specific charges: murder, waging war against the state, forgery, terrorism under Indian law. Different elements, different legal theory, same underlying events.
India requested extradition in 2020. Rana fought it for nearly five years — a magistrate's certification, a habeas corpus petition, a Ninth Circuit appeal, and finally the Supreme Court, where his last argument was that extradition to India would expose him to torture under the UN Convention Against Torture. The Ninth Circuit's own language cut through the argument cleanly: the crimes charged in India, the court found, have elements independent from what he'd already been tried for in the US.
Justice Kagan rejected his final stay application on March 6, 2025. On April 4, the full Court dismissed his review petition.

## SEGMENT 7 — THE HANDOVER
On April 9, 2025, US Marshals transferred custody of Tahawwur Rana to India's National Investigation Agency. He landed in Delhi the following evening on a special flight, and was formally arrested the moment he stepped off the plane.
US Secretary of State Marco Rubio's statement was blunt: Rana would, in his words, face justice for his role in the Mumbai attacks. It was, on paper, the culmination of a legal fight that had run since 2020 — and unofficially, one India had been pursuing since the 2011 verdict left it without the one outcome it actually wanted.

## SEGMENT 8 — WHERE THINGS STAND NOW
Since his arrival, Rana has been held in Tihar Jail. Early questioning by Mumbai Police and the NIA reportedly ran eight to ten hours a day; investigators have collected voice and handwriting samples to match against intercepted Headley communications. Solicitor General Tushar Mehta was appointed to lead the prosecution personally — a signal of how significant Delhi considers this case. A supplementary chargesheet was filed in July 2025.
As of the most recent reporting available, no trial verdict has been reached. This is, deliberately, an unfinished story — which is worth saying on camera rather than pretending otherwise.

## CLOSING
Every other figure in this series so far — the trainers, the gunmen, the handlers in a Karachi control room — built their role around violence directly. Rana's role was different, and in some ways harder to prosecute precisely because of that: he gave a terrorist a cover story, an office, a business card. A friendship from a Punjab boarding school, still intact forty years later, that happened to be exactly what a reconnaissance operation needed.
An American jury looked at that and found it wasn't quite enough to convict him of the attack itself. An Indian court is now deciding whether it was enough to convict him of everything else.
