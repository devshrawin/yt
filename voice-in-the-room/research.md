## PART 1 — BACKGROUND RESEARCH

### Who he is
**Sajid Abdulmajid Mir**, also known as Sajid Majid, Sajid Majid Chaudhary, and by the alias **"Uncle Bill"**. Born 1976 (some records say 1978) to a middle-class Punjabi family in Lahore, where his father ran a small textile business. He earned degrees from the University of Punjab in English Literature and Islamic Studies, and speaks Arabic, Urdu and English fluently — a profile that reads more like a mid-level bureaucrat than the popular image of a militant. He married the daughter of a retired Pakistani army officer and had two sons. Height listed as 5'5"–5'6" (165–168 cm).

### Role in Lashkar-e-Taiba
Mir rose to become **deputy head of LeT's international operations wing**, effectively running the group's "foreign affairs" — recruiting, handling, and deploying operatives with Western passports who could move through Europe, North America, and India without triggering the scrutiny a Pakistani national would. Per investigators, he had already run operatives into Virginia, Europe, and Australia — some later captured — before the Mumbai operation. This is the detail that separates him from a purely regional commander: he was, functionally, LeT's project manager for international operations.

### The Headley connection and the 2005 India trip
Mir became **David Coleman Headley's primary handler**, providing financial and logistical support and directing Headley's target selection during his Mumbai scouting trips (2006–2008). Before that, in 2005, Mir personally entered India using a fake passport and identity, posing as a spectator for an India-Pakistan cricket match in New Delhi, staying roughly 15 days and personally reconnoitering Mumbai — meaning the man who later ran the Mumbai control room had walked the city himself.

### The chief planner
The FBI's own wanted notice describes him as the **"chief planner"** of the attacks, "directing preparations and reconnaissance, and as one of the key controllers during the attacks." This is the direct link to your 26/11 and Rana/Headley scripts: Mir is the "voice from Karachi" both of those scripts already named. He was physically present in the LeT control room in Pakistan during the siege, in phone contact with the attackers, watching Indian television coverage in real time to relay troop and hostage positions.

### The Nariman House detail — a new, specific beat for this script
According to Headley's 2011 Chicago court testimony (during the Rana trial), Mir directed the two attackers at Nariman House to use mattresses as cover and ambush NSG commandos descending the staircase during the rescue operation. Rana, listening in, reportedly praised Mir's tactics so highly he compared him to **Khalid bin Walid**, a legendary 7th-century Arab military commander — a compliment Headley then relayed back to Mir. This single exchange is a genuinely chilling, specific, sourced moment for your cold open or a mid-video beat: the handler being complimented on tactics that killed hostages, mid-siege, by phone.

### After 26/11: still operating
Mir was not caught. Headley later told the FBI he worked with agents in a failed attempt to lure Mir out of Pakistan for arrest after his own 2009 capture. Despite being a wanted man, Mir was confident enough in his protection to visit LeT operations chief **Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi** at least twice in Adiala Jail in Rawalpindi — once in early 2009, and again in September 2009, according to testimony from LeT operative Zabiuddin Ansari. During the second visit, Lakhvi reportedly told the gathered militants he'd "struck a deal with the government" and no further arrests would follow. In 2010, Mir began planning a further attack on the **Nashik Police Academy**, roughly 120 miles east of Mumbai, according to Indian counter-terror officials.

### The US indictment and bounty
On April 21, 2011, Mir was indicted in the US District Court in Chicago on charges including conspiracy to injure property of a foreign government, providing material support to terrorists, killing a US citizen outside the United States, and bombing places of public use. The FBI placed a **$5 million bounty** on him under the Rewards for Justice program — the same program, same scale of reward, as top al-Qaeda figures. The FBI also states he underwent plastic surgery after the attacks, complicating identification efforts.

### "Declared dead," then "resurrected" — the FATF timeline
This is the strongest, strangest thread in the whole story, and it should anchor your video's back half.
- **December 2021** — Pakistan told international partners, including via the US State Department's own *Country Reports on Terrorism 2020*, that Mir was believed dead, or at minimum "remained free" and unaccounted for. Western governments didn't believe it and demanded proof.
- **April 21, 2022** — Pakistan suddenly announced his arrest — almost exactly a month before a critical FATF (Financial Action Task Force) plenary in Berlin that would decide whether Pakistan stayed on the international "grey list" for terror financing.
- **May 16, 2022** — in an unusually fast trial, just over three weeks after his "arrest," an anti-terrorism court in **Gujranwala** convicted Mir on terror-financing charges: three consecutive sentences (six months, eight years, seven years) totaling roughly **15.5 years**, plus a fine of about Rs 420,000. The proceeding was held *in camera* — no media allowed — at Lahore's high-security Kot Lakhpat Jail. The Counter Terrorism Department didn't even notify media of the conviction; it only surfaced via a leaked court order and lawyer sources weeks later.
- Pakistan cited this exact conviction to the FATF as evidence of progress. **Pakistan was removed from the FATF grey list later in 2022.**
- India abstained from formally commenting, since the verdict was never officially announced in Islamabad — there was, on paper, nothing to react to.

The sequencing here is the whole story in miniature: dead when it was convenient, alive and convicted exactly when a financial regulator needed proof of "progress," convicted only on financing charges unrelated to Mumbai itself — never tried for the 166 deaths his own indictment describes him as directing.

### China's shield at the UN
Despite the conviction, the United States and India jointly proposed designating Mir a global terrorist under the UN Security Council's 1267 Al-Qaeda Sanctions Committee — which would trigger an asset freeze, travel ban, and arms embargo. **China blocked the proposal at least three times** (reported holds in September 2022, then repeated blocks through 2023), using the procedural "technical hold" mechanism available to permanent Security Council members. China used the identical mechanism in the same period to block designations of LeT's Abdul Rehman Makki, JeM's Abdul Rauf Azhar, and Hafiz Saeed's son Hafiz Talha Saeed — a pattern, not a one-off. India's UN representative called the pattern evidence that "something is genuinely wrong with the global counter-terrorism architecture."

### The poisoning reports (December 2023) — and the information vacuum since
Unconfirmed but widely reported: Mir was allegedly poisoned in **Dera Ghazi Khan Central Jail**, having been transferred there some months earlier, and reportedly airlifted by the Pakistan Army to CMH Institute of Medical Sciences in **Bahawalpur** on ventilator support. Indian intelligence sources speculated he might have staged illness or worse to avoid any future extradition pressure. **No outlet has been able to confirm his status since** — this needs to be stated plainly on screen. As of the most recent research available for this script, there is no verified public update on whether Mir survived, remains imprisoned, or his current whereabouts.

### The ISI question
A French counter-terrorism magistrate who investigated Mir was quoted concluding he was "a high-ranking officer in the Pakistani Army and apparently also was in the ISI" — an assessment echoed by investigators in multiple countries per reporting citing French terrorism expert Jean-Louis Bruguière's findings. This is allegation-level, not court-proven, and the script should frame it that way — consistent with how your first video handled the same ISI-tolerance question.

### Caveats to state in the video
- Mir has never faced trial — anywhere — specifically for the Mumbai attacks. His only conviction is for unrelated terror financing.
- The "chief planner" characterization comes from the FBI and US State Department; it has not been tested in an adversarial courtroom the way, say, Rana's case was.
- The poisoning reports are unconfirmed and should be flagged as such, not stated as fact.
- Birth year is disputed (1976 vs. 1978) between Wikipedia and Jamestown's research profile — use "mid-to-late 1970s" rather than picking one.

## PART 4 — FULL SCRIPT

### THE VOICE IN THE ROOM: The Sajid Mir Story
**Runtime target:** 18–22 minutes
**Tone:** Investigative, with a deliberate undercurrent of dark irony — the story's engine is bureaucratic absurdity (a "dead" man resurrected for a financial audit) sitting on top of real atrocity. Don't let the irony soften the horror; let them sit side by side.

**[VISUAL: Black screen. A phone icon pulses. Two silhouettes, unlabeled — one in a hotel stairwell graphic, one behind a desk with a TV glow.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
During the siege of Nariman House in November 2008, a voice on the phone from Pakistan gave two gunmen a tactical instruction: pile the mattresses at the top of the stairs, and ambush the commandos as they come down.

Later, over the same phone line, one of his own colleagues would relay him a compliment for it — comparing him to one of history's most famous military commanders.

Six hostages were murdered in that building, including a rabbi and his wife.

The man giving the instructions was never in the room. He's spent most of the seventeen years since as, officially, either dead, free, or — very briefly, and very conveniently — in prison. This is the story of Sajid Mir: the voice in the room, who was never actually in the room.

**[VISUAL: Map pin on Lahore. A simple document-style graphic: "University of Punjab — English Literature & Islamic Studies."]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Sajid Abdulmajid Mir doesn't look like the terrorist archetype most documentaries reach for. Born in the mid-to-late 1970s to a middle-class Lahore family — his father ran a small textile business — Mir studied English Literature and Islamic Studies at the University of Punjab, married the daughter of a retired army officer, and had two sons. He spoke fluent Arabic, Urdu, and English.

That fluency, and that unremarkable profile, is precisely what made him useful. Within Lashkar-e-Taiba, Mir rose to become deputy head of the group's international operations — effectively its foreign-affairs desk. His specialty wasn't fighting. It was recruiting and running operatives who could pass through Western airports without a second look: men LeT had already deployed into Virginia, Europe, and Australia before Mumbai was ever on the table.

#### SEGMENT 2 — THE MAN WHO SCOUTED THE SCOUT (4:00–7:00)

**[VISUAL: Map line, Lahore → New Delhi, 2005.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Before Mir ever sent someone else to scout Mumbai, he scouted it himself. In 2005, travelling on a fake passport under a false identity, Mir entered India posing as a spectator for an India-Pakistan cricket match in New Delhi. He stayed roughly fifteen days — and quietly reconnoitered Mumbai in person.

Only afterward did he recruit David Coleman Headley, the American-Pakistani operative whose story we covered in a previous video, and become his direct handler. Mir financed Headley's scouting trips, directed his target selection, and — per Headley's own later testimony — pushed him for increasingly specific material: hotel floor plans, conference-room schedules, ballroom layouts at the Taj.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — ProPublica, "Mumbai Attacks: David Coleman Headley, Part 2"; Jamestown Foundation profile]**

By the time ten gunmen landed on Mumbai's shore in November 2008, Mir wasn't managing a plan from a distance. He'd walked the ground himself, three years earlier.

#### SEGMENT 4 — UNCAUGHT (10:30–13:00)

**[VISUAL: Map, Adiala Jail pin appears with a small "visited" icon, twice.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Mir wasn't caught in the aftermath. When David Headley was finally arrested in Chicago in October 2009, he told FBI agents everything he knew about his handler — and even cooperated in a failed attempt to lure Mir out of Pakistan so he could be arrested. It didn't work. Mir remained free.

In fact, he remained confident. According to testimony from fellow LeT operative Zabiuddin Ansari, Mir visited Lashkar's operations chief, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi, at Adiala Jail in Rawalpindi — not once, but twice, in early and then September 2009 — despite being a wanted fugitive with a bounty on his head. During the second visit, Lakhvi reportedly told the gathered militants he'd "struck a deal with the government" and no further arrests would follow.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — PBS Frontline/ProPublica, "Four Disturbing Questions About the Mumbai Terror Attack"]**

The following year, Mir was already planning his next operation — a strike on the Nashik Police Academy, roughly 120 miles from Mumbai, according to Indian counter-terror officials. It was never carried out. But the ambition tells you something: this wasn't a man in hiding.

#### SEGMENT 5 — DEAD, ON PAPER (13:00–15:30)

**[VISUAL: A document graphic reading "Country Reports on Terrorism 2020 — U.S. Department of State" fades in, then a red stamp: "STATUS: DECEASED (disputed)."]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
By 2011, the US had indicted Mir in a Chicago federal court and put a five-million-dollar bounty on his head — the same scale of reward reserved for senior al-Qaeda leadership.

And then, on paper, he vanished. Pakistani authorities let it be understood — without ever formally confirming it — that Mir had died, a claim that surfaced clearly in the US State Department's own December 2021 terrorism report, which noted pointedly that Pakistan "did not take action" against him, "believed to remain free." Western governments didn't buy the death story. They wanted proof. None came.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Dec 2021: Pakistan implies Mir is dead. No proof offered."]**

#### SEGMENT 6 — RESURRECTED, RIGHT ON SCHEDULE (15:30–19:00)

**[VISUAL: A calendar graphic. "April 21, 2022" appears, then ticks forward day by day toward a highlighted date: "FATF Plenary, Berlin — June 2022."]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Here is where the story turns genuinely strange.

On April 21, 2022 — almost exactly one month before a critical Financial Action Task Force meeting in Berlin, the meeting that would decide whether Pakistan stayed on the international "grey list" for failing to control terror financing — Pakistan suddenly announced that Sajid Mir had been arrested.

Three weeks later, on May 16, an anti-terrorism court in Gujranwala convicted him. Not for Mumbai. For terror financing — a lesser, unrelated charge. Three consecutive sentences totaling roughly fifteen and a half years. The trial was held in camera, inside Lahore's high-security Kot Lakhpat Jail. No media allowed. Pakistan's own Counter Terrorism Department didn't even publicly announce the conviction — it surfaced only through leaked court documents and lawyers speaking to reporters weeks later.

**[LOWER THIRD: "May 16, 2022 — convicted in a closed courtroom, no media present, exactly before the FATF's Berlin review"]**

Pakistan then presented this exact conviction to the FATF as evidence of counter-terrorism progress. It worked. Later that year, Pakistan came off the grey list.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Dawn; Al Jazeera; EFSAS Commentary, July 2022; Jamestown Foundation]**

Dead when no one was asking questions. Alive, arrested, tried and convicted within three weeks — the exact three weeks a financial regulator needed proof of action. Convicted of financing terrorism, never once tried for directing an attack that killed 166 people, six of them American citizens.

#### SEGMENT 7 — PROTECTED AT THE TOP (19:00–21:30)

**[VISUAL: UN Security Council chamber icon. A red "HOLD" stamp appears over a document three separate times, each with a date.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Conviction in hand, the United States and India jointly moved to have Mir designated a global terrorist by the United Nations Security Council — a formal step that would trigger an asset freeze, travel ban, and arms embargo.

China blocked it. Then blocked it again. Then blocked it a third time, across 2022 and 2023, using a procedural tool available to any permanent Security Council member: the technical hold. In the same window, China used the identical mechanism to block designations of three other Pakistan-based LeT and JeM figures. India's own UN representative said publicly that the pattern showed, in his words, that something was "genuinely wrong with the global counter-terrorism architecture."

**[LOWER THIRD: "China blocked Mir's UN terrorist designation at least three times, 2022–2023"]**

A man the FBI calls the Mumbai attack's "chief planner," convicted in Pakistan of a lesser charge in a closed courtroom, remains — as a matter of formal UN Security Council status — not internationally sanctioned.

**[VISUAL: The unlabeled silhouette from the cold open, now surrounded by a fading paper trail of documents — indictment, court order, UN hold notice — each dissolving into blank pages.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Every other figure in this series left a paper trail that eventually closed — a sentence, an extradition, a testimony on the record. Sajid Mir's paper trail does something else.
