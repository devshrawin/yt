# THE VOICE IN THE ROOM: The Sajid Mir Story
### Long-form documentary script — YouTube (investigative, dark irony)

**Runtime target:** 11–13 minutes
**Tone:** Investigative, with an undercurrent of dark irony — a "dead" man resurrected for a financial audit, sitting on top of real atrocity. Restructured from the original draft (Part 4 of `research.md`) to `../SCRIPT_RULES.md`; facts from Parts 1–3.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:30)

**[VISUAL: Split screen — Nariman House stairwell on one side, a Karachi control room with a TV glow on the other. A phone line pulses between them.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
November 2008. Nariman House, Mumbai. Two gunmen are holding a Jewish centre, and commandos are about to come down the stairs. [PAUSE] On a phone line from Pakistan, a calm voice gives them an order. Pile the mattresses at the top of the stairs. Wait for the commandos.

Six hostages died in that building. Among them were a rabbi and his wife. Later, according to court testimony, a friend of the plotters compared the man on the phone to one of history's great generals. [PAUSE] The compliment was passed along to him.

The man on the phone was never in the room. His name is Sajid Mir, and the FBI calls him the chief planner of 26/11. Since then, Pakistan has described him as free, as dead, and — for one very convenient season — as a convict. [PAUSE] So where is the man who ran Mumbai by phone? And why has no court ever tried him for it?

Everything here comes from the FBI's wanted notice, sworn testimony in a Chicago courtroom, and Pakistan's own court order. [PAUSE] His status, it turns out, changes whenever someone needs it to.

**[TITLE CARD: THE VOICE IN THE ROOM]**

## SEGMENT 1 — THE QUIET RECRUITER (1:30–2:45)

**[VISUAL: Region map, pin on Lahore. FBI wanted photo, captioned as pre-2008.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
He was born in Lahore in the mid-to-late 1970s. Records disagree on the year. His father ran a small textile business. Mir studied English literature and Islamic studies at the University of the Punjab. He married a retired army officer's daughter, and they had two sons. [PAUSE] On paper, he could have been a mid-level civil servant.

That was the point. Inside Lashkar-e-Taiba, he rose to deputy head of international operations — in effect, its foreign desk. He didn't fight. He recruited. His speciality was people with the right passports, men who could cross a Western airport without a second look. According to investigators, he had already run operatives into Virginia, Europe and Australia before Mumbai. [PAUSE] Some of them were caught. He never was.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Sajid Mir — deputy head, Lashkar-e-Taiba international operations"]**

## SEGMENT 2 — THE MAN WHO SCOUTED THE SCOUT (2:45–4:15)

**[VISUAL: Region map. Route Lahore → Delhi → Mumbai, 2005.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Before he sent anyone to Mumbai, he went himself. In 2005, according to investigators, Mir entered India on a fake passport, posing as a fan at an India–Pakistan cricket match in Delhi. He stayed about fifteen days. [PAUSE] Not all of them at the cricket. Part of that time, he spent looking at Mumbai.

Then he found a better pair of eyes. David Headley — the man from our last file — became his operative, and Mir became his handler. He funded the trips. He chose the targets. Headley later testified that Mir kept pushing for more detail: floor plans, routes, the layout of the Taj. [PAUSE] Headley was the camera. Mir decided where it pointed.

When ten gunmen came ashore in November 2008, the man directing them had already walked the ground.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — ProPublica, "Mumbai Attacks: David Coleman Headley, Part 2"; Jamestown Foundation]**

---

## SEGMENT 3 — THE VOICE FROM KARACHI (4:15–5:45)

**[VISUAL: The control room from the cold open, now labelled "Karachi control room". Nariman House on the city map.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
For sixty hours, Mir sat in a control room in Karachi — the room from our second file. He was on the phone to the gunmen. Indian news channels played on the screens, broadcasting where the commandos were moving. [PAUSE] He was watching the same coverage as everyone else.

The FBI's wanted notice calls him the attack's "chief planner", and "one of the key controllers during the attacks."

At Nariman House, Headley later testified in Chicago, Mir told the two gunmen to barricade the stairs with mattresses and wait for the commandos. Tahawwur Rana, hearing about it, compared the tactic to Khalid bin Walid, a legendary seventh-century general. He asked Headley to pass the praise to Mir. [PAUSE] Six hostages had died in that building. The compliment was delivered anyway.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Testimony: Mir directed the Nariman House ambush by phone"]**

---

## SEGMENT 4 — UNCAUGHT (5:45–7:00)

**[VISUAL: Region map. Adiala Jail pin with two "visit" marks; then Nashik.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Headley was arrested in October 2009. He told the FBI everything he knew about his handler. He even helped agents try to lure Mir out of Pakistan. [PAUSE] Mir didn't come.

He didn't need to hide, either. Zabiuddin Ansari, a Lashkar operative later arrested by India, told investigators that Mir twice visited Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi in Adiala Jail, in Rawalpindi. Lakhvi was Lashkar's operations chief, then on trial for Mumbai. On the second visit, in September 2009, Lakhvi reportedly told the men around him he had "struck a deal with the government." No more arrests would follow.

A year later, Indian counter-terror officials say, Mir was planning his next strike: the police academy at Nashik, north-east of Mumbai. It never happened. [PAUSE] But this was not a man on the run.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — ProPublica / PBS Frontline, "Four Disturbing Questions About the Mumbai Terror Attack"]**

---

## SEGMENT 5 — DEAD, ON PAPER (7:00–8:15)

**[VISUAL: A case-file checklist — indictment, reward, the "believed dead" claim — with a red stamp: "DEAD? NO PROOF".]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In April 2011, a federal grand jury in Chicago indicted him — conspiracy, material support to terrorists, the killing of US citizens abroad. The United States offered up to five million dollars for information leading to him. The FBI says he may since have had plastic surgery. [PAUSE] The face on the poster may no longer be his.

Then, quietly, he died. At least, on paper. Pakistan let its partners understand that Mir was dead, without ever confirming it. Western governments asked for proof. [PAUSE] None came. The US State Department's terrorism report, released in December 2021, still described him as believed to remain free.

Dead to some. Free to others. [PAUSE] It depended on who was asking.

**[LOWER THIRD: "2021 — Pakistan implies Mir is dead. No proof offered."]**

---

## SEGMENT 6 — RESURRECTED, ON SCHEDULE (8:15–10:00)

**[VISUAL: A progress bar — April 2022 arrest, May 2022 conviction, June 2022 FATF plenary in Berlin, October 2022 off the grey list.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Then someone started asking who mattered.

The Financial Action Task Force is the global watchdog on terror financing. It had kept Pakistan on its grey list since 2018, and its next key review was due in Berlin in June 2022.

In April 2022, the dead man was arrested. [PAUSE] Weeks later, on May 16, an anti-terrorism court convicted him. Not for Mumbai. For financing terrorism. Fifteen and a half years, across three sentences. The trial was held behind closed doors, inside Lahore's Kot Lakhpat Jail. No reporters. No announcement. According to Dawn, the verdict surfaced only weeks later, through court papers and lawyers.

Pakistan put the conviction in front of the watchdog. [PAUSE] It worked. That October, Pakistan came off the grey list.

Dead while nobody was asking. Alive, arrested and convicted in the weeks a regulator needed proof. [PAUSE] And never once tried for the attack that killed 166 people.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Dawn; Al Jazeera; EFSAS Commentary, July 2022; Jamestown Foundation]**

---

## SEGMENT 7 — PROTECTED AT THE TOP (10:00–11:00)

**[VISUAL: Two columns — "US + India propose" and "China's hold" — filling in, with a stamp: "NOT SANCTIONED".]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
With a conviction on file, the United States and India asked the UN Security Council to list Mir as a global terrorist. That would mean an asset freeze, a travel ban and an arms embargo.

China put the request on hold. [PAUSE] Then it did it again. It was a technical hold, a procedural brake that any permanent member can pull. In the same period, China used it on other Pakistan-based militants, including Hafiz Saeed's son. India's diplomat at the UN said the pattern showed that something was "genuinely wrong with the global counter-terrorism architecture."

So the man the FBI calls the chief planner of 26/11 stands convicted of a lesser crime, in a closed court. [PAUSE] As of the latest reporting, he is still not on the UN's sanctions list.

**[LOWER THIRD: "China blocked Mir's UN listing — September 2022 and June 2023"]**

---

## SEGMENT 8 — THE LAST SIGHTING (11:00–11:50)

**[VISUAL: Region map. A line traces Lahore → Dera Ghazi Khan → Bahawalpur, fading to a dotted grey.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
In December 2023, reports surfaced in Pakistan and India. Mir had been poisoned inside Dera Ghazi Khan Central Jail, they said, and flown by the army to a military hospital in Bahawalpur, on a ventilator. None of it has been confirmed. Indian intelligence sources suggested, without proof, that it might have been staged — a way to vanish before anyone could ask for him again.

Since then, nothing verified has surfaced. [PAUSE] Alive or dead, in prison or free — as of the latest reporting, nobody outside Pakistan can say.

**[LOWER THIRD: "December 2023 — unconfirmed poisoning reports. No verified update since."]**

---

## CLOSING (11:50–12:45)

**[VISUAL: The control room from the cold open. The screens go dark one by one; the phone line fades.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Every other name in this story ended on the record. Headley took a plea. Rana was extradited to face trial. [PAUSE] Sajid Mir's record does something else. It opens and closes on command. Dead when that was convenient. Convicted when a regulator needed proof. Tried only for a crime that never required saying the word Mumbai in open court.

In 2008 he was the voice in the room, giving orders to men he never stood beside. [PAUSE] Years later, he is still only a voice. And the room he spoke from has never been opened.

**[END CARD: Sources listed on screen for 8 seconds]**
