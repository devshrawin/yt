# THE MERCENARY: The David Headley Story — original draft (Part 4 of the brief, verbatim)

**Runtime target:** 20–24 minutes
**Tone:** Investigative and accountability-focused. The through-line: one man, three masters (drug agents, spies, terrorists), and a system that was warned again and again.

---

#### COLD OPEN (0:00–1:15)

**[VISUAL: Three overlapping circles labeled "DEA," "Lashkar-e-Taiba," "ISI." A single silhouette stands where all three meet.]**

Between 2002 and 2009, one man was, at various points, a paid informant for the US Drug Enforcement Administration, a trained operative of Lashkar-e-Taiba, and, by his own testimony, a contact of Pakistani intelligence officers. He scouted the targets for 26/11. He scouted a second attack in Denmark. He met with al-Qaeda figures. And along the way, the United States government was warned about him again and again.

At least five times.

This is the story of David Coleman Headley. Not the terrorist mastermind of legend, but something more unsettling: a man so useful to so many that nobody stopped him in time.

**[TITLE CARD: THE MERCENARY]**

#### SEGMENT 1 — TWO NAMES, TWO WORLDS (1:15–4:00)

**[VISUAL: Map. Pins on Washington D.C., Lahore, Hasan Abdal, Philadelphia.]**

He was born Daood Sayed Gilani in Washington, D.C., in 1960. His father was a Pakistani broadcaster; his mother, an American from a wealthy Philadelphia family. By infancy he was in Pakistan, and as a boy he attended Cadet College Hasan Abdal — the same military prep school where he met a classmate named Tahawwur Rana, whose story we covered in our last video.

At seventeen he left for Philadelphia to live with his mother, and took her maiden name: Headley. A US anti-terror official would later describe him with a striking eye colour mismatch, a fast-talking charm, and a talent for moving between worlds. The same official summed him up in one line: a mercenary, not ideologically driven.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Born Daood Gilani, Washington D.C., 1960 — became David Coleman Headley"]**

#### SEGMENT 2 — THE DRUG YEARS AND THE DEA (4:00–7:00)

**[VISUAL: Timeline graphic: 1988 Frankfurt → 1997 Manhattan → 2002 contract ends.]**

By the late 1980s Headley was running heroin between Pakistan and the West. In 1988, German police in Frankfurt found two kilos hidden in a suitcase. He cooperated with US drug agents, gave up his partners, and served four years while his co-defendant received ten.

The pattern repeated. In 1997, the DEA caught him in a Manhattan hotel sting over another heroin deal. Again he cooperated, and this time he became a confidential informant, travelling to Pakistan on the agency's behalf. He served roughly fifteen months instead of what could have been nine years.

After 9/11, his handlers reportedly had him gather intelligence on extremists as well as drug networks. The DEA says its last contract with him ended in March 2002.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — ProPublica/NBC; PBS Frontline; EFSAS study paper]**

That same year, he walked into a Lashkar-e-Taiba event in Pakistan.

#### SEGMENT 3 — BECOMING LASHKAR (7:00–10:00)

**[VISUAL: Map pins Muzaffarabad and Mansehra; five small course icons appear in sequence.]**

According to the US Department of Justice, Headley attended **five Lashkar-e-Taiba training courses between 2002 and 2005**. Accounts of his first, a basic course in Muzaffarabad, describe it as overseen by Lashkar's operations chief, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi. This is the same camp pipeline we walked through in the first video of this series, seen now through the eyes of someone who went through it while, on paper, still being someone the US government trusted.

In late 2005, three Lashkar members told him to travel to India and conduct surveillance. He changed his name on paper to David Headley precisely because a Western name would draw less suspicion at Indian border checks. Lashkar handler Sajid Mir, the same voice from the Karachi control room in our 26/11 video, was already discussing sending him to India.

**[LOWER THIRD: "2002–2005: Five LeT courses — Muzaffarabad and Mansehra"]**

#### SEGMENT 4 — FIVE TRIPS TO MUMBAI (10:00–14:00)

**[VISUAL: Mumbai map. Pins for Taj, Oberoi-Trident, Leopold, Nariman House, CST light up one by one, each with a small camera icon.]**

Headley opened the Mumbai branch of Tahawwur Rana's First World Immigration Services and used it as his cover story. Between 2006 and 2008 he made five surveillance trips to the city, filming and marking targets with GPS. He stayed at the Taj Mahal Palace and the Oberoi-Trident, hotels that would become the main stage of the siege. He cultivated Mumbai's social scene, befriending Rahul Bhatt, the son of a well-known Bollywood filmmaker, who guided him around the city.

In later court testimony he said Lashkar had also weighed other targets, including the Siddhivinayak Temple and a conference of defence scientists at the Taj. He also said two earlier sea-borne attempts, in September and October 2008, failed because of accidents before the November attack succeeded.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — US DOJ sentencing release; Gulf News/Deccan Herald reports on Headley's 2016 testimony]**

By the time ten men landed on Mumbai's shoreline on November 26, they were navigating with his work.

#### SEGMENT 5 — FIVE WARNINGS (14:00–18:30)

**[VISUAL: A five-item checklist fills in one by one over a dark background: 2001, 2002, 2005, 2007, April 2008. A sixth item, December 2008, appears in a different colour with the label "after the attack."]**

Now the part that makes this story a scandal rather than just a crime.

A review led by the US Director of National Intelligence, reported by ProPublica and the Washington Post, found at least five separate cases in which relatives or associates warned authorities that Headley was training with or working for Pakistani militants.

In 2005, his American wife told FBI agents in New York, after a domestic dispute, that he was an active Lashkar militant who had trained in its camps. The FBI "looked into" the tip. Nothing came of it publicly.

In 2007, his Moroccan wife, Faiza Outalha, twice met US Embassy officials in Islamabad. She showed them photographs of their stays at the Taj and the Oberoi. Her reported words to them: "he's either a terrorist, or he's working for you." She wasn't taken seriously.

Further tips surfaced in 2001, 2002 and April 2008. A last one arrived in December 2008, a month after the attack.

**[LOWER THIRD: "At least five warnings — 2001 to April 2008 — before 26/11"]**

US officials have said the tips were too general to connect Headley to a specific plot, and that had they known the specifics they would have told India. Headley was not stopped until October 2009, eleven months after Mumbai, and only after British intelligence flagged his contacts with al-Qaeda figures in Europe. That is the documented failure: missed warnings. Whether he was ever deliberately protected is speculation, and we should say so.

#### SEGMENT 6 — AFTER MUMBAI: DELHI, DENMARK, AL-QAEDA (18:30–20:30)

**[VISUAL: Map lines from Chicago to Copenhagen and to Delhi; a separate dotted line to Waziristan.]**

Headley did not stop after 26/11. In 2009 he scouted more targets in India, including Delhi's National Defence College. And in Denmark, he carried out a similar mission against the newspaper Jyllands-Posten, which had published the Prophet Muhammad cartoons.

By his own later testimony, he also worked with al-Qaeda-linked commander Ilyas Kashmiri, who gave him money for airfare and expenses, on further plots including targeting Chabad Houses in India.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Deccan Herald reporting on Headley's 2016 deposition; Wikipedia]**

Notice the pattern. Headley never had one loyalty. He had a skill set, a passport that opened doors, and buyers.

#### SEGMENT 7 — ARREST, PLEA, AND THE DEAL (20:30–23:00)

**[VISUAL: O'Hare airport pin. A document graphic reads "12 counts. Plea. Cooperation."]**

On October 3, 2009, FBI agents arrested Headley at Chicago's O'Hare Airport as he prepared to fly to Pakistan carrying about thirteen surveillance videos for his handlers.

In March 2010 he pleaded guilty to all twelve counts and began cooperating. The deal removed the possibility of the death penalty and included a promise that he would not be extradited to India. In January 2013, Judge Harry Leinenweber sentenced him to thirty-five years. The judge called him a terrorist, and acknowledged that life imprisonment would have been appropriate but for the significant value of his cooperation.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Jan 24, 2013 — 35 years — no extradition to India"]**

Headley then testified against his old friend Tahawwur Rana, whose story we told in the previous video.

#### SEGMENT 8 — THE INDIAN COURT (23:00–25:30)

**[VISUAL: A split screen: a video-link frame on one side (no face), an Indian courtroom silhouette on the other.]**

In December 2015, Headley appeared by video link from an undisclosed location in the United States before a Mumbai court and asked for a pardon in exchange for testimony. The court agreed, making him an approver. The pardon does not affect his US sentence.

In February and March 2016, he became the first foreign terrorist to testify before an Indian court. He named Pakistani intelligence officers he said were involved with Lashkar's handlers. He described the ISI as supporting several militant groups. And he made a claim about Ishrat Jahan, a woman killed in a 2004 police encounter, that became a political storm in India.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Business Standard, Al Jazeera, Daijiworld/Deccan Herald reporting, Feb–Mar 2016]**

A note of caution the video should say out loud: Headley is a convicted trafficker, a former informant and a self-described deceiver. His testimony is valuable evidence, but it is his account.

#### CLOSING (25:30–27:00)

**[VISUAL: The three circles from the cold open, slowly separating until the silhouette stands alone.]**

David Headley is serving thirty-five years in an undisclosed US prison. In 2018 there were reports he was attacked by fellow inmates. India has asked repeatedly for access to him. The United States has, so far, declined to let him come.

The uncomfortable lesson of his story isn't that a single man fooled everyone. It's that the warnings were real, they came from people close to him, and the systems built to catch someone like him were looking at drugs, at intelligence, and at terrorism, but rarely at all three at the same time. He was the seam between them.
