# THE MERCENARY: The David Headley Story
### Long-form documentary script — YouTube (investigative accountability story)

**Runtime target:** 14–16 minutes
**Tone:** Investigative, accountability-focused. Restructured from the original draft (`script_draft_v0.md`) to `../SCRIPT_RULES.md`; facts from `research.md`. Through-line: one man, three masters, and a system that was warned again and again.

---

## COLD OPEN (0:00–1:30)

**[VISUAL: O'Hare Airport. Then three overlapping circles labelled "DEA", "Lashkar-e-Taiba", "ISI", a single silhouette where they meet.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
October 2009. Chicago's O'Hare Airport. A man with one green eye and one brown is about to board a flight to Pakistan. In his luggage are about thirteen surveillance videos, meant for the men who sent him. [PAUSE] He never boards. FBI agents stop him first.

Eleven months earlier, his scouting had guided ten gunmen into Mumbai. He had scouted a second attack in Denmark. And for years before that, he had been working for the United States government. [PAUSE] A paid informant for its drug agency.

In the same decade he trained with Lashkar-e-Taiba. By his own testimony, he took money from an officer of Pakistan's intelligence service. And the United States was warned about him again and again. [PAUSE] At least five times.

So how does a man flagged that often walk through every door? Everything here comes from the record — his guilty plea, US court filings, and his own testimony to an Indian court. [PAUSE] His name was David Coleman Headley. And he lived in the gaps between the agencies meant to catch him.

**[TITLE CARD: THE MERCENARY]**

## SEGMENT 1 — TWO NAMES (1:30–3:00)

**[VISUAL: World map. Pins on Washington D.C. and Lahore, then Hasan Abdal and Philadelphia.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
He was born Daood Gilani, in Washington, D.C., in 1960. His father was a Pakistani broadcaster. His mother was an American from a wealthy Philadelphia family. [PAUSE] Then came Pakistan. He was still an infant.

As a boy he went to Cadet College Hasan Abdal, a military prep school in Punjab. There he met a classmate named Tahawwur Rana — the front man of our last file. At seventeen, Gilani moved back to America to live with his mother. He spoke Urdu and English. He could be charming in both.

Two countries. Two languages. [PAUSE] Eventually, two names. He would later take his mother's maiden name, and become David Headley. One US counter-terrorism official, quoted by ProPublica and NBC, summed him up in a single line: "a mercenary, not ideologically driven." [PAUSE] Hold on to that word. It explains almost everything that follows.

**[LOWER THIRD: "Born Daood Gilani · Washington, D.C., 1960 — later David Coleman Headley"]**

---

## SEGMENT 2 — THE INFORMANT'S BARGAIN (3:00–5:00)

**[VISUAL: Timeline over a world map — 1988 Frankfurt, 1997 Manhattan, 2002 last DEA contract.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
The first thing Headley learned was that getting caught was negotiable.

In 1988, German police in Frankfurt found two kilos of heroin in his luggage, carried from Pakistan. He cooperated with American drug agents and gave up his partners. He served about four years. [PAUSE] His co-defendant got ten.

Nine years later, according to court records, it happened again. The Drug Enforcement Administration caught him in a heroin sting at a Manhattan hotel. Again he traded. This time he became a confidential informant, and the agency sent him to Pakistan on its behalf. He served about fifteen months instead of a possible nine years.

After the September 11 attacks, his handlers reportedly had him gather intelligence on extremists, not only drug networks. The DEA says his last contract ended in March 2002. [PAUSE] Every time the system caught him, it found a use for him instead.

That same year, he walked into a Lashkar-e-Taiba gathering in Pakistan.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — ProPublica/NBC; PBS Frontline; EFSAS study paper]**

---

## SEGMENT 3 — THE SECOND EMPLOYER (5:00–7:00)

**[VISUAL: Region map. Pins on Muzaffarabad and Mansehra; five course markers light up in sequence.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
He had come to hear a lecture by Hafiz Saeed, Lashkar's founder. He stayed. According to the US Department of Justice, Headley went on to attend five Lashkar training courses between 2002 and 2005.

The first was a basic course in Muzaffarabad, in Illegally Occupied Pakistani Kashmir. Accounts describe it as overseen by Lashkar's operations chief, Zaki-ur-Rehman Lakhvi. Later training took him to the Mansehra area. It is the same camp pipeline from the first file in this series. [PAUSE] Except this recruit held an American passport — and had, until recently, been on the payroll of an American agency.

That passport was the point. In late 2005, three Lashkar members told him to go to India and conduct surveillance. The next year, he legally made the Western name his own. [PAUSE] A David Headley draws fewer questions at an Indian border than a Daood Gilani.

His handler was Sajid Mir. Three years later, Mir would be one of the voices in the Karachi control room, directing the gunmen by phone.

**[LOWER THIRD: "2002–2005 · Five Lashkar-e-Taiba courses · Muzaffarabad and Mansehra"]**

---

## SEGMENT 4 — THE TOURIST (7:00–9:15)

**[VISUAL: South Mumbai map. Pins on the Taj, Oberoi-Trident, Leopold Café, Nariman House and CST light up one by one, each with a small camera mark.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Every spy needs a reason to be somewhere. Headley's came from his oldest friend. He opened a Mumbai branch of Tahawwur Rana's immigration consultancy, and used it as his cover story.

Between 2006 and 2008 he made five surveillance trips to the city. He filmed. He walked the routes. He marked targets with GPS. [PAUSE] He did not hide in cheap rooms. He checked into the Taj Mahal Palace and the Oberoi-Trident — the hotels that would become the main stage of the siege.

He found a gym, and friends — among them the son of a well-known Bollywood filmmaker, who showed him around the city. [PAUSE] To anyone watching, he was an American businessman enjoying Mumbai.

In court years later, Headley testified that Lashkar had weighed other targets too — a famous temple, and a conference of defence scientists at the Taj. He also said two earlier attempts by sea had failed, in September and October 2008, because of accidents. [PAUSE] Two attempts failed. The third did not.

When ten men came ashore in Mumbai that November, they were working from his reconnaissance.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — US DOJ sentencing release; Gulf News and Deccan Herald on Headley's 2016 testimony]**

---

## SEGMENT 5 — FIVE WARNINGS (9:15–12:00)

**[VISUAL: A dark checklist fills in one item at a time — 2001, 2002, 2005, 2007, April 2008. A sixth, December 2008, appears in red, labelled "after the attack".]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
This is where the story stops being only a crime.

A review led by the US Director of National Intelligence, reported by ProPublica and The Washington Post, found at least five separate warnings. People close to Headley told American authorities he was training with, or working for, Pakistani militants. [PAUSE] Some of them were his own wives.

In 2005, after a domestic dispute, his American wife went to FBI agents in New York. She told them he was an active Lashkar militant who had trained in its camps. The FBI says it looked into the tip. [PAUSE] Nothing came of it.

In 2007, his Moroccan wife, Faiza Outalha, went to the US Embassy in Islamabad. Twice. She brought photographs of their stays at the Taj and the Oberoi. Her words to the officials, as reported: "He's either a terrorist, or he's working for you." [PAUSE] She was not taken seriously.

There were more tips — in 2001, in 2002, and in April 2008. The last one arrived in December 2008. [PAUSE] A month after the attack.

US officials say the tips were too general to link him to a specific plot, and that with specifics they would have warned India. Headley was stopped only in October 2009, after British intelligence flagged his contacts with al-Qaeda figures in Europe. [PAUSE] The documented failure is missed warnings. That anyone protected him on purpose has never been shown.

**[LOWER THIRD: "At least five warnings — 2001 to April 2008 — all before 26/11"]**

---

## SEGMENT 6 — THE NEXT BUYER (12:00–13:15)

**[VISUAL: World map. Lines from Chicago to Copenhagen and to Delhi.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Mumbai did not end his work. It raised his price.

In 2009 he was back in India, scouting new targets, including Delhi's National Defence College. Then he turned to Denmark. His target was Jyllands-Posten, the newspaper that had published cartoons of the Prophet Muhammad. He scouted its offices the way he had scouted the Taj.

By his own later testimony, he was now also working with Ilyas Kashmiri, an al-Qaeda-linked commander, who paid for his flights. [PAUSE] Drug agents. Then Lashkar. Now al-Qaeda. Headley never had one loyalty. He had a skill set, a passport that opened doors — and buyers.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Deccan Herald on Headley's 2016 deposition; US DOJ]**

---

## SEGMENT 7 — THE LAST TRADE (13:15–14:45)

**[VISUAL: O'Hare pin, then a document graphic — "12 counts · Guilty plea · Cooperation".]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
Which brings us back to O'Hare. When FBI agents stopped him in October 2009, Headley did what he had always done. [PAUSE] He made a deal.

In March 2010 he pleaded guilty to all twelve counts and began to cooperate. The plea took the death penalty off the table. And it promised he would never be extradited to India. [PAUSE] Then he took the stand against his oldest friend, Tahawwur Rana.

In January 2013, Judge Harry Leinenweber sentenced him to thirty-five years. "Mr. Headley is a terrorist," the judge said. Prosecutors had argued that life in prison would have been right — but for the value of his cooperation. [PAUSE] Twenty-five years after Frankfurt, the bargain still worked.

**[LOWER THIRD: "January 24, 2013 · 35 years · no extradition to India"]**

---

## SEGMENT 8 — THE WITNESS (14:45–16:15)

**[VISUAL: Split frame — a blank video-link window on one side, an Indian courtroom outline on the other.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
India never got Headley in person. It got him on a screen. In December 2015 he appeared by video link, from an undisclosed location in America, before a special court in Mumbai. He offered testimony in exchange for a pardon. The court accepted. [PAUSE] He became an approver — a witness for the prosecution. The pardon changes nothing about his US sentence.

In February and March 2016, he became the first foreign terrorist reported to testify before an Indian court. He named Pakistani intelligence officers he said worked alongside Lashkar's handlers. He described the ISI as backing several militant groups. He also made a hearsay claim about Ishrat Jahan, a young woman killed in a 2004 police encounter. [PAUSE] It became a political storm.

Here is the catch. Every one of those claims rests on the word of a man who trafficked, informed and lied for a living. [PAUSE] His testimony is evidence. It is also his account.

**[CITATION CARD: Sources — Business Standard and Al Jazeera, December 2015; Deccan Herald, February–March 2016]**

---

## CLOSING (16:15–17:30)

**[VISUAL: The three circles from the cold open drift apart until the silhouette stands alone.]**

**NARRATOR (V.O.):**
David Headley is serving thirty-five years in an undisclosed US prison. In 2018 there were reports that fellow inmates attacked him. India has asked again and again to question him in person. When Rana was flown to Delhi in 2025, it reportedly asked once more. [PAUSE] He has stayed in America.

The lesson of his story isn't that one man fooled everyone. The warnings were real. They came from the people closest to him. But the agencies built to catch a man like him were each watching one thing — drugs, or spies, or terror. [PAUSE] Never all three at once.

He was the seam between them. [PAUSE] And a seam is exactly where things come apart.

**[END CARD: Sources listed on screen for 8 seconds]**
